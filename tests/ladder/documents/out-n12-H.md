## Q1. Who are you and what do you do?

We are Halewater Commons Trust, a charitable incorporated organisation constituted in 2016 and based in Kelverton, a post-industrial town of about 41,000 people in northern England. We have six paid staff (4.2 full-time equivalent) and about 25 regular volunteers. Our expenditure in the last filed financial year was £214,000 and we have two years of filed accounts.

We run open-access youth sessions for 11 to 19 year olds, a weekly supper club, and a small advice desk for families, on the east side of town. The youth sessions run three evenings a week in Marlpit, the ward on the east bank of the river, where 38 per cent of households sat in the lowest income decile at the 2021 census. In October 2024 we opened a second delivery site on Ferry Bank, the estate north of the old goods yard; 31 young people attended in its first term. The youth work is led by Priya Raval, our delivery coordinator since 2019, who holds a level 3 youth work qualification and personally runs four of the six weekly sessions in Marlpit. Our board is chaired by Delroy Ferguson, a retired further education lecturer and trustee since 2018, who lives in Ferry Bank.

We work alongside Dunmore Sixth Form College, a mile from the Marlpit site; Barrowfield Youth Justice Team, which referred nine young people to us between April 2024 and March 2025; St Aidan's Parish Hall in Marlpit; Wharfside Boathouse on the Ferry Bank side of the river; Redgate Catering on the Marlpit industrial estate; and Northgate Minibus Hire.

## Q2. What is the problem, and how do you know?

The problem is in two named places: Marlpit ward and the Ferry Bank estate, either side of the old goods yard and the river. In Marlpit, 38 per cent of households were in the lowest income decile at the 2021 census. On Ferry Bank, six of the nine young people referred to us by Barrowfield Youth Justice Team between April 2024 and March 2025 were resident on the estate.

The symptom we see is young people leaving school in these two places without a next step attached. The causes we intend to act on are three, and our evidence is our own delivery records.

First, the gap between a referral and an enrolment. In 2024-25 we referred 27 school-leavers to Dunmore Sixth Form College and 18 enrolled; nine did not. The college is a mile from the Marlpit site, so the barrier is not distance but the unaccompanied step itself.

Second, the absence of a structured bridge for those not ready to apply. When we ran a twelve-week progression course, Second Chances, in Marlpit from January to April 2025, 24 young people started, 19 finished and 11 moved into a college place, an apprenticeship or paid work. That course has not run since, so the bridge exists only when funded.

Third, adults on Ferry Bank with no route into a role. Our cook-and-eat course Night Kitchen ran twice on Ferry Bank in 2025, in February and June; 22 adults completed and 15 went on to volunteer at the supper club. Ferry Bank had no such offer before October 2024.

We do not hold independent needs data for these two places beyond the census figure above, and we do not claim any.

## Q3. What will you do?

Over 18 months, in Marlpit and on Ferry Bank only, we will do four things in sequence.

Open-access youth sessions continue as the front door: six weekly sessions across the two sites, four of them run by Priya Raval, the rest by sessional youth workers. These identify who is approaching a leaving point.

Second Chances runs three twelve-week cohorts, one every six months, alternating between St Aidan's Parish Hall in Marlpit and the Ferry Bank site, delivered by Priya Raval with sessional support. We will recruit 24 to each cohort, 72 places in total, drawn from the sessions and from Barrowfield Youth Justice Team referrals.

Accompanied progression to Dunmore Sixth Form College: each cohort member and each other school-leaver we work with is walked through application, visit and enrolment by a named worker rather than referred on paper. This addresses the nine young people who did not enrol in 2024-25.

Night Kitchen runs three more times on Ferry Bank, delivered by our staff and volunteers, with completers offered a volunteer role at the weekly supper club at St Aidan's Parish Hall. The supper club continues weekly: 78 evenings across 18 months, hall hire at £40 an evening, food from Redgate Catering at £2.40 a head, budgeted on a planning assumption of 20 heads per sitting, so 1,560 heads. Off-site activity uses 21 minibus trips from Northgate Minibus Hire at £95 a trip, all picking up in Marlpit or on Ferry Bank, and 18 days at Wharfside Boathouse at £120 a day across two summers.

## Q4. What will change, and how will you know?

Our targets, scaled from our own recorded rates:

From 72 Second Chances places across three cohorts, we expect 57 completions and 33 young people into a named next step — a place at Dunmore Sixth Form College, an apprenticeship, or paid work. Our 2025 cohort ran at 19 of 24 completing and 11 of 24 progressing; three cohorts at that rate give 57 and 33.

From accompanied progression, we expect 40 school-leavers taken through the Dunmore application and 27 enrolled, against 27 referred and 18 enrolled in 2024-25.

From three Night Kitchen runs, we expect 33 adults completing (22 completed across two runs in 2025) and 22 moving into a named volunteer role at the supper club.

We will measure by named-person record: who started, who finished, where they went, confirmed with the receiving provider. Delroy Ferguson reports the figures to the board quarterly.

We would count this as not having worked if fewer than 20 of the 72 Second Chances places end in a named next step, if the Dunmore enrolment rate falls below the 18-in-27 we already achieve, or if fewer than 12 Night Kitchen completers take a volunteer role.

## Q5. What happens after the grant?

Three things continue and we can say who owns each.

The accompanied progression route to Dunmore Sixth Form College continues. It is a practice, not a project: our workers walk young people in rather than posting a referral. It costs staff time already committed to the youth sessions and no separate budget. Priya Raval owns it.

The volunteer pipeline from Night Kitchen into the supper club continues. Each cohort produces volunteers who staff the club, which reduces its paid-staff need. The supper club's cash cost is hall hire at £40 an evening plus food at £2.40 a head — about £88 an evening at 20 heads, £4,576 a year at 52 evenings — which we fund from our unrestricted income and continue to seek locally.

The Second Chances curriculum, written up after three cohorts, is owned by the board and can be run again by our own staff at sessional cost when funds allow.

We are not able to name a committed successor funder, and we will not imply one.
