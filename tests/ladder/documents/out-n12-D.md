## Q1. Who are you and what do you do?

Halewater Commons Trust is a charitable incorporated organisation, constituted in 2016, working on the east side of Kelverton, a post-industrial town of about 41,000 people in northern England. We have six paid staff (4.2 full-time equivalent) and around 25 regular volunteers. Expenditure in the last filed financial year was £214,000, and we have two years of filed accounts.

Our work sits in two places. Marlpit is the ward on the east bank of the river, where open-access youth sessions for 11 to 19 year olds run three evenings a week, and where the weekly supper club uses St Aidan's Parish Hall at £40 an evening. Ferry Bank is the estate north of the old goods yard, where we opened a second delivery site in October 2024. Summer activity in 2024 and 2025 used Wharfside Boathouse on the Ferry Bank side of the river, 18 days across the two summers at £120 a day.

The youth work is led by Priya Raval, delivery coordinator since 2019, who holds a level 3 youth work qualification and personally runs four of our six weekly sessions in Marlpit. The board is chaired by Delroy Ferguson, a retired further education lecturer and a trustee since 2018, who lives in Ferry Bank and signs off the safeguarding review each year. We also run a small advice desk for families.

We already work with young people referred by Barrowfield Youth Justice Team, which sent nine to us between April 2024 and March 2025, six of them living in Ferry Bank. Since 2023 we have referred school-leavers to Dunmore Sixth Form College, a mile from our Marlpit site. Food for the supper club comes from Redgate Catering on the Marlpit industrial estate at £2.40 a head. Transport is bought from Northgate Minibus Hire at £95 a trip.

## Q2. What is the problem, and how do you know?

Young people in Marlpit and Ferry Bank leave school and stop moving. At the 2021 census, 38 per cent of Marlpit households sat in the lowest income decile. Ferry Bank is the estate our newest site serves and the address of six of the nine young people sent to us by Barrowfield Youth Justice Team in 2024-25.

Our own numbers show where the loss happens. In 2024-25 we referred 27 school-leavers to Dunmore Sixth Form College and 18 enrolled, so nine did not. When we ran the twelve-week Second Chances course in Marlpit from January to April 2025, 24 started, 19 finished and 11 moved into a college place, an apprenticeship or paid work. That is the shape of the problem: the referral itself is the easy part, and people fall out between the referral and the first fortnight of the placement.

The causes we intend to act on are the ones we can see in that gap. First, nobody walks alongside a young person after the referral is made, so a missed induction or a lost enrolment letter ends the attempt. Second, the crossing matters. The college is a mile from our Marlpit site and further from Ferry Bank, and our transport record shows every one of the 14 minibus trips in 2024-25 picked up in Marlpit, so Ferry Bank young people start further from everything. Third, our Ferry Bank site is new and its 31 first-term attenders are a reach figure, not a progression figure; we have no structured progression offer there yet. Fourth, adults in Ferry Bank have appetite but no route: 22 completed the Night Kitchen cook-and-eat course in February and June 2025 and 15 went on to volunteer at the supper club, with nothing after that.

## Q3. What will you do?

We will run a progression programme in both neighbourhoods over 18 months, from 1 March 2027 to 31 August 2028.

- Three cohorts of 16 young people aged 16 to 19, 48 in total, on a twelve-week course built from Second Chances. Cohorts one and three run in St Aidan's Parish Hall, Marlpit; cohort two runs at our Ferry Bank site, north of the old goods yard, so that estate gets its own cohort rather than a bus ride to somebody else's. Priya Raval designs and leads the course; a new part-time progression worker delivers alongside her and carries the caseload.
- Recruitment comes from our three weekly Marlpit sessions, the Ferry Bank site and Barrowfield Youth Justice Team, which already refers to us.
- Each cohort has four off-site visits, 12 trips in total with Northgate Minibus Hire at £95, picking up in Ferry Bank as well as Marlpit. Visits go to the college, to a workplace and to a training provider.
- Every young person gets a named next step written down in week six, and eight weeks of follow-through after the course ends: enrolment paperwork, the first day, the second week. This is the part that was missing.
- Two runs of Night Kitchen in Ferry Bank, 12 adults each, 24 in total. Completers are offered a volunteer shift at the supper club, food hygiene training and a supported approach to a local employer or a catering course.
- Twelve summer days at Wharfside Boathouse across the two summers, used as the bridge week between cohorts and as recruitment for the next one.
- Course meals come from Redgate Catering at £2.40 a head.

## Q4. What will change, and how will you know?

Outcomes are people reaching a named next step, not attendance.

- 48 young people start; 38 complete twelve weeks. Second Chances finished 19 of 24, and we are holding roughly that rate.
- 22 of the 48 reach a college place, an apprenticeship or paid work, of which at least 12 enrol at Dunmore Sixth Form College. Second Chances converted 11 of 24, and 18 of 27 referrals enrolled in 2024-25; the follow-through worker is what should lift the number.
- 18 of those 22 are still in the placement eight weeks after starting. We will record this by phone or in person, not by assumption.
- 24 adults start Night Kitchen; 20 complete; 14 take a volunteer role at the supper club. This is a role in our own organisation, not an independent destination, and we count it separately. Six adults go further, to a food hygiene qualification or paid or trial work with a local employer.

Measurement is a single spreadsheet held by the delivery coordinator, one row per person, with the destination, the provider, the start date and the eight-week check. The chair reviews it quarterly.

We would count this as not having worked if fewer than 15 of the 48 young people reach a named next step, or if fewer than 10 of the 22 are still in place at eight weeks. We will say so if that happens.

## Q5. What happens after the grant?

Three things continue. The week-six written next step and the eight-week follow-through become standard practice in all our youth sessions; that is a habit, not a budget line, and Priya Raval owns it. The Ferry Bank cohort slot continues twice a year at our existing site. The supper club volunteer route out of Night Kitchen continues, owned by the supper club coordinator.

Running the twelve-week course once a year without us costs about £5,600: 12 evenings of St Aidan's Parish Hall at £40, four minibus trips at £95, meals from Redgate Catering, and coordinator time already in our core budget. That is affordable from our existing income, and the trustees have agreed it becomes a standing item rather than a project. We will also ask the referring agencies that use the route to contribute to transport.
