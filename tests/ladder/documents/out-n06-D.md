## Q1. Who are you and what do you do?

Halewater Commons Trust is a charitable incorporated organisation, constituted in 2016, working in Kelverton, a post-industrial town of about 41,000 people in northern England. We have six paid staff (4.2 full-time equivalent) and around 25 regular volunteers. Total expenditure in our last filed financial year was £214,000, and we have two years of filed accounts.

Our work sits on the east side of town, in Marlpit, the ward on the east bank of the river. We run open-access youth sessions for 11 to 19 year olds three evenings a week there, a weekly supper club at St Aidan's Parish Hall in Marlpit, hired at £40 an evening, and a small advice desk for families. In the year to March 2025 the supper club served 46 sittings.

The youth work is led by our delivery coordinator, Priya Raval, in post since 2019 and holding a level 3 youth work qualification. She personally runs four of the six weekly sessions in Marlpit. Since 2023 we have referred school-leavers to Dunmore Sixth Form College, a mile from our Marlpit site; 27 young people were referred in 2024-25 and 18 enrolled. Between January and April 2025 we ran a twelve-week progression course in Marlpit called Second Chances: 24 started, 19 finished, and 11 moved into a college place, an apprenticeship or paid work.

## Q2. What is the problem, and how do you know?

Young people in Marlpit leave school and stall. Marlpit is the ward on the east bank, where 38 per cent of households sat in the lowest income decile at the 2021 census, and it is the ward our three weekly youth sessions serve.

The causes we intend to act on are the ones we watch in the room on a Tuesday night, and the ones our own numbers show.

First, the gap between referral and enrolment. We referred 27 school-leavers to Dunmore Sixth Form College in 2024-25 and 18 enrolled. Nine did not. A referral is a phone number, not a place; nobody walks the mile with them, and nobody is there in week three when it gets hard.

Second, the absence of anything between an open-access youth session and a college application. Second Chances closed that gap for one cohort in early 2025 and 11 of the 24 who started reached a college place, an apprenticeship or paid work. It ran once because it was funded once.

Third, cost and distance. Every one of the 14 minibus trips we bought from Northgate Minibus Hire between April 2024 and March 2025 picked up in Marlpit, because getting out of Marlpit costs money that families in the lowest income decile do not have spare.

The evidence is our own records: referral and enrolment counts, Second Chances completion and destination figures, and the pick-up pattern on every trip we have paid for.

## Q3. What will you do?

We will run Second Chances three times in Marlpit, as a structured twelve-week progression course, and add the walking-alongside part that our referral figures say is missing.

Cohort structure: three cohorts of 16, 48 young people aged 16 to 19 in total. Cohort 1 April to July 2027, cohort 2 September to December 2027, cohort 3 January to April 2028. Recruitment is from the three open-access youth sessions in Marlpit, from the families using our advice desk, and from word of mouth around the supper club.

Each cohort runs two evenings a week at St Aidan's Parish Hall in Marlpit, hired at £40 an evening: 72 hire evenings across the grant. Priya Raval designs and leads the course, as she did in 2025, at half her time; a sessional youth worker delivers the second evening and covers her open-access sessions so the existing three nights a week do not shrink.

Weeks 1 to 4 are basics: attendance habit, literacy and numeracy refresh, what the options actually are. Weeks 5 to 8 are exposure: eight minibus trips per cohort with Northgate Minibus Hire, 24 trips at £95, to Dunmore Sixth Form College a mile away for taster sessions and open days, and to local employers and training providers we will approach in the first two months of the grant. Weeks 9 to 12 are the application itself, done in the room: forms completed, interviews rehearsed, enrolment dates in the diary.

Then the part we do not currently fund. For six months after each cohort ends, Priya or the sessional worker keeps monthly contact with every participant who has taken a next step, and goes with them to the college or provider in week one and week three if they want company. Participant costs cover bus fares, interview clothing and course materials.

## Q4. What will change, and how will you know?

Of the 48 young people who start, we expect 38 to complete twelve weeks and 24 to reach a named next step: an enrolled place at Dunmore Sixth Form College or another provider, a started apprenticeship, or paid work of 16 hours a week or more. Of those 24, we expect 18 to still be in that place three months later.

These targets come from what we have already done. Second Chances 2025 gave 19 completions from 24 starters and 11 progressions, so we are holding the completion rate at roughly four in five and the progression rate a little under one in two across three times the volume. The college route gave 18 enrolments from 27 referrals; the six-month follow-up is our attempt to lift that ratio rather than repeat it.

Measurement is a single spreadsheet, one row per young person, kept by the delivery coordinator: attendance by week, next step named with provider and start date, and a recorded contact at one, three and six months after the course ends. Destinations are confirmed with the provider, not taken on trust. We will pay for two half-days of external evaluation to check the record and write up what worked.

We would count this as not having worked if fewer than 16 of the 48 reach a named next step, or if completion falls below 29 of 48. Either would tell us the course does not travel beyond the one cohort that produced it, and we would say so.

## Q5. What happens after the grant?

Two things continue. The first is the twelve-week course itself, once a year rather than three times, run at St Aidan's Parish Hall on the evenings we already hire and staffed from the coordinator time we already fund. Twelve weeks of hire at £40 is £480, and one cohort needs four minibus trips at £95, £380. Participant costs for 16 young people are about £960. That is roughly £1,820 a year, which the trustees have the capacity to carry within a £214,000 turnover and which we will build into the annual budget as a standing line rather than a project.

The second is the follow-up practice: the monthly contact and the walk to the door in week one. That costs staff attention rather than money, and it becomes part of how the delivery coordinator runs the youth work. Our board owns both commitments; the coordinator owns the delivery.

We will also leave the college and the employers we visit with a named contact and a pattern of arriving every year, which is worth more than a written agreement.
