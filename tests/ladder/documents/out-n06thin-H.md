## Q1. Who are you and what do you do?

We are Halewater Commons Trust, a charitable incorporated organisation constituted in 2016 and based in Kelverton, a post-industrial town of about 41,000 people in northern England. We have six paid staff (4.2 full-time equivalent) and about 25 regular volunteers. Our expenditure in the last filed financial year was £214,000, and we have two years of filed accounts.

Our work is on the east side of town, in Marlpit. We run open-access youth sessions for 11 to 19 year olds, a weekly supper club, and a small advice desk for families. We have used St Aidan's Parish Hall as a venue, and Northgate Minibus Hire as a transport supplier. We hold a relationship with Dunmore Sixth Form College, and we have previously run a programme called Second Chances. Priya Raval is one of the people involved in our work.

The people we already work with are, in the main, Marlpit families who come through one door and use more than one thing: a parent at the advice desk whose son is in the youth session, a young person who eats at the supper club on the same evening. That overlap is the asset this application is built on.

## Q2. What is the problem, and how do you know?

The problem we intend to act on is that young people in Marlpit reach 16, 17 or 18 in contact with us and then arrive at no named next thing. We see them in open-access sessions, we know their families through the advice desk, and then the contact thins out. We are being plain with the Foundation about the limits of our evidence: we hold no formal tracking data on where our young people go after they leave our sessions, and we are not going to present attendance records as though they were destination records. The absence of that tracking is itself part of the problem we are asking you to fund us to fix.

The causes we can speak to from our own operation are three. First, our youth offer is open-access, which is its strength for reach and its weakness for progression: nobody in the building has the job of taking a named young person to a named course or employer. Second, the step into post-16 provision is a step out of Marlpit, and we already buy in minibus transport when we need to move young people, which tells us that getting out of the neighbourhood is a cost and a barrier rather than a given. Third, our relationship with Dunmore Sixth Form College sits at organisational level and has not been turned into a route that an individual young person walks along with support. We ran Second Chances, so we know we can build something more structured than drop-in; we have not yet built it around progression.

Why Marlpit and not the next area over: Marlpit is where our staff, our volunteers, our venue and our existing family relationships are. We do not claim knowledge of other parts of Kelverton that we do not work in.

## Q3. What will you do?

We will convert part of our open-access offer in Marlpit into a progression route, over 18 months, in three cohorts of 12 young people aged 16 to 19, drawn from the young people and families we already see: 36 young people in total.

Sequence, per cohort (six months each, running consecutively):

1. Identification, weeks 1–3. Our youth workers and our advice desk staff identify 12 young people from existing contact in Marlpit. This happens in our own sessions and at the advice desk.
2. One-to-one planning, weeks 2–6. A progression worker (0.6 FTE, funded by this grant) meets each young person individually and writes down one named next step and the steps to reach it.
3. Group sessions, weeks 4–24. Weekly group sessions at St Aidan's Parish Hall, delivered by the progression worker with sessional youth workers and volunteers. Across the whole grant we budget 108 sessions at St Aidan's.
4. Visits out, weeks 8–20. Three minibus visits per cohort — nine across the grant — using Northgate Minibus Hire, so that a course or a workplace is somewhere the young person has physically been. Our relationship with Dunmore Sixth Form College is the route we will use first; we will approach other providers but do not name what we have not yet agreed.
5. Handover, weeks 20–26. The progression worker accompanies each young person to the point of application, enrolment or start, and records who took them on.

Volunteer training (two rounds) equips volunteers to hold one-to-one check-ins so the practice does not sit with one post. Priya Raval will be involved in the programme's oversight within our existing team.

## Q4. What will change, and how will you know?

Intended outcomes and numbers, from 36 young people worked with:

- 36 written progression plans, each naming one specific next step and the provider of it.
- 24 young people reaching a named next step — a course, a qualification, a job, a return to education, or a named service that takes them on. That is two thirds of 36, and it is a target we are prepared to be judged against.
- 36 young people making at least one visit out of Marlpit to a provider site.

We will measure by individual record, not headcount: for each young person, the plan, the named provider, the date of enrolment or start, and a follow-up contact three months later. We will build this tracking from scratch, because we do not have it now, and we will report the gaps honestly.

We would count this work as not having worked if fewer than 12 of the 36 reach a named next step, or if we end the grant with attendance figures and no destination records — that is, if we have run more sessions and still cannot say where anyone went.

## Q5. What happens after the grant?

What continues is the practice, not the post: a named next step written down for every 16-plus young person we work with in Marlpit, and a record of whether they got there. Our existing youth sessions and advice desk continue as they do now, funded as they are now; the progression practice becomes part of how they are run. Trained volunteers, not the grant-funded worker, hold the one-to-one check-ins.

Ownership sits with our trustees and our existing staff team. The running cost without the grant is the cost of volunteer training and the recording system, which we estimate at the £1,800 training line and £2,000 evaluation line in this budget being replaced by a smaller annual commitment met from our own unrestricted income — our expenditure last year was £214,000, so this is a proportionate ongoing charge rather than a new dependency. We will not claim we have secured future funding for a continued post, because we have not.
