## Q1. Who are you and what do you do?

Halewater Commons Trust is a charitable incorporated organisation, constituted in 2016, based in Kelverton, a post-industrial town of about 41,000 people in northern England. Registration is current, two years of accounts are filed, and expenditure in the last filed year was GBP 214,000. We apply as sole applicant and accountable body. Website: halewatercommons.org.uk.

We have six paid staff (4.2 FTE) and about 25 regular volunteers. We run open-access youth sessions for 11 to 19 year olds, a weekly supper club and a small family advice desk on the east side of town. Youth sessions run three evenings a week in **Marlpit**, the ward on the east bank of the river, where 38 per cent of households sat in the lowest income decile at the 2021 census. In October 2024 we opened a second site on the **Ferry Bank** estate; 31 young people attended in its first term. The supper club uses St Aidan's Parish Hall, Marlpit, at GBP 40 an evening, serving 46 sittings in the year to March 2025, with food from Redgate Catering at GBP 2.40 a head. Summer activity in 2024 and 2025 used Wharfside Boathouse, hired for 18 days at GBP 120 a day. Night Kitchen, a cook-and-eat course, ran twice in Ferry Bank in 2025; 22 adults completed and 15 went on to volunteer at the supper club.

Youth work is led by Priya Raval, delivery coordinator since 2019, who holds a level 3 youth work qualification and runs four of the six weekly Marlpit sessions. The board is chaired by Delroy Ferguson, a retired further education lecturer, trustee since 2018 and a Ferry Bank resident, who signs off the annual safeguarding review. We work in these two wards because that is where our sessions, volunteers and referral routes already are.

## Q2. What is the problem, and how do you know?

In 2024-25 we referred 27 school-leavers to Dunmore Sixth Form College, a mile from our Marlpit site. Eighteen enrolled; nine did not. We made those referrals ourselves, so the gap is ours to explain.

Our course data repeats the pattern. Second Chances, a twelve-week progression course, ran in Marlpit from January to April 2025: of 24 starters, 19 finished and 11 moved into a college place, apprenticeship or paid work. Barrowfield Youth Justice Team referred nine young people between April 2024 and March 2025, six of them living in Ferry Bank.

Our records show a drop at the transition rather than a shortage of places: a young person can complete a course with us and still not appear at enrolment. Practical barriers cluster at that point — travel the mile from Marlpit or across the river from Ferry Bank, appointment timings, first-week costs — and for young people with prior negative experience of education, an institutional appointment attended alone is easy to avoid. Our records also stop at referral: we hold no data on whether those who enrolled were still attending twelve weeks later. *Through the Door* closes both gaps by putting a named person alongside the young person across the boundary, and by measuring the far side of it.

## Q3. What will you do?

*Through the Door* rebuilds Second Chances as a 12-week course followed by a named first-term handover, in Marlpit and Ferry Bank only, over 18 months. Priya Raval leads delivery at 0.4 FTE; a new progression keyworker (0.8 FTE) carries the caseload, with 0.2 FTE cover for clashing enrolment appointments.

| Phase | Months | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Set-up | 1-2 | Draft handover protocol and note template; agree route with the college and referrals with youth justice |
| Cohort 1, Marlpit | 2-8 | Course at St Aidan's Parish Hall, enrolment accompaniment, 12-week contact |
| Cohort 2, Ferry Bank | 6-13 | Course on the estate, accompaniment, 12-week contact, protocol revised |
| Cohort 3, Marlpit | 11-18 | Course, accompaniment, 12-week contact |
| Close | 16-18 | Final funnel review, signed adoption of protocol, coordinator time absorbed into existing sessions |

Recruitment is by one-to-one conversation at our sessions and via youth justice referrals, confirming age 16 to 19, residence in the two wards and no stable post-16 place. Each cohort, capped at 22 starters, has a weekly group session plus fortnightly one-to-ones covering course choice, applications, travel routes, money, timetable planning and practice of the enrolment conversation. Each cohort makes two visits to Dunmore using Northgate Minibus Hire at GBP 95 a trip, picking up in Marlpit.

Before completion each young person is allocated a named keyworker and co-writes a signed handover note, passed to the college, or to the employer where the next step is an apprenticeship or job. The keyworker attends the enrolment or interview appointment in person. Twelve weeks of scheduled contact follow, tapering from weekly to fortnightly, fixing transport, paperwork and attendance problems. Existing sessions and the supper club remain the informal contact point, so no new premises are needed. Funnel reviews fall at months 6, 11, 16 and 18. Total participants: 66.

## Q4. What will change, and how will you know?

We count the funnel at every join and treat week-12 retention, not enrolment, as the outcome that matters.

| Indicator | Baseline | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Starters across three cohorts | 24 in the 2025 Marlpit cohort | 66 |
| Completing the course | 19 of 24 | 50 of 66 |
| Accompanied in person to an appointment | Not previously delivered | 44 of 50 completers |
| Handover notes passed to receiver | Not previously delivered | 44 |
| Enrolled, apprenticed or in paid work | 11 of 24; 18 of 27 referrals enrolled | 38 of 66 |
| Still in place at week 12 | Not previously recorded | 29 of 38 |
| Protocol adopted in writing by the college | Not previously in place | Signed by month 12 |
| Funnel review meetings | Not previously held | 4 |

Methods: attendance registers, keyworker contact log, note register with transfer dates, and receiving-institution confirmation corroborated by keyworker contact where consent allows. Declining contact intensity for at least two thirds of those retained is our measure of growing independence.

Failure is enrolment without retention; if we reach 38 enrolments and lose most by week 12, we will report it. Risks are non-formalisation by the college, caseloads exceeding capacity, and dependence on college data. We mitigate by opening negotiation in month 1, capping cohorts at 22 and sequencing them so intensive periods do not overlap at full load, and recording week-12 status from two sources.

## Q5. What happens after the grant?

What continues is the handover protocol and written referral note: keyworker allocation, accompaniment to the appointment, the note passed to the receiver, and twelve weeks of contact. The Trust owns the protocol, template and funnel data; the design is for Dunmore Sixth Form College to hold it as its standard intake route for Marlpit and Ferry Bank referrals, signed by month 12.

Running it costs coordinator time absorbed into existing sessions, about half a day a week, plus marginal transport and venue costs and the college's own admissions staffing. Coordinator time comes from existing funded staffing and marginal costs from our general delivery budget; the college carries its side. The dedicated keyworker post does not continue without further funding, and no source is held; the protocol is designed to survive at lower intensity without it. At month 18 we retain a written protocol, a tested template, 18 months of funnel data, a signed intake agreement and a trained coordinator and volunteer base, with no premises or capital required.

This application addresses the Round 3 priorities of work rooted in a named place, progression to a defined next step, and something that outlasts the grant. It will be submitted before 17:00 GMT on Friday 27 November 2026.
