## Q1. Who are you and what do you do? Your organisation, the people you already work with, and the geography you work in.

Halewater Commons Trust is a charitable incorporated organisation constituted in 2016. We operate on the east side of Kelverton, a post-industrial town of about 41,000 people in northern England. Our team comprises six paid staff (4.2 full-time equivalent) and about 25 regular volunteers. In the last filed financial year, our total organizational expenditure was £214,000, and our accounts have been filed for two consecutive years.

Our core delivery is based entirely in Marlpit, a single ward situated on the east bank of the river in Kelverton. We deliver three distinct services from our Marlpit base:
- Open-access youth sessions running three evenings a week for young people aged 11 to 19.
- A weekly supper club providing regular hot meals and a consistent meeting space.
- A small advice desk that assists local families with practical issues, welfare navigation, and local support.

Through these three activities, we work week in, week out with young people who grow up in severe economic hardship, alongside their families. At the 2021 census, 38 per cent of households in Marlpit sat in the lowest income decile. Our evening youth sessions provide the primary point of contact for teenagers across the ward who are otherwise detached from formal services, training providers, or extra-curricular opportunities.

Over the past decade, we have established direct links between our open-access sessions and formal education. Since 2023, we have run an active referral route for local school-leavers to Dunmore Sixth Form College, which is located one mile from our Marlpit site. In the 2024–25 academic year, we referred 27 young people to Dunmore Sixth Form College, and 18 of those young people subsequently enrolled. 

Between January and April 2025, we piloted Second Chances, an intensive twelve-week progression course based at our Marlpit venue. Out of 24 young people who began that pilot course, 19 completed it, and 11 progressed directly into a sixth form place, an apprenticeship, or paid employment. We now seek funding to build on this specific track record and establish a permanent, repeatable progression pipeline from our Marlpit base.

## Q2. What is the problem, and how do you know? Be specific about place and population. Describe the causes you intend to act on, not only the symptoms, and say what your evidence for them is.

The problem we address is the acute drop-off between compulsory schooling and sustained post-16 education, training, or employment for 16-to-19-year-olds living in Marlpit. 

The evidence of place and economic distress is documented: Marlpit is on the east bank of Kelverton's river, separated physically from the town centre and major training facilities. According to the 2021 census, 38 per cent of households in Marlpit are in the lowest national income decile. In our advice desk work and supper club, we see how household financial strain deprives young people of basic stability, transport funds, and the practical guidance needed to transition out of secondary education.

Through our three-evening-a-week youth club, we observe that the symptom—young people hanging around on streets without destinations at 16 or 17—stems from three concrete, local causes:

1. Lack of transition support between open-access contact and institutional intake. Young people in Marlpit frequently disengage from education at 16 because mainstream application and enrolment procedures require administrative confidence, interview experience, and digital access that their households cannot easily provide. Without structured guidance, initial intent does not translate into enrolment. Our 2024–25 referral data proves this gap: while we identified and referred 27 young people to Dunmore Sixth Form College, 9 did not complete enrolment because they lacked continuous transition casework.

2. Absence of intermediate, small-group readiness training. Young people who have spent months out of education or training cannot step straight into a formal college course or workplace without rebuilding basic study habits, communication routines, and daily punctuality. When pushed into formal settings without preparation, they drop out before completing their courses.

3. Geographic and institutional detachment. Dunmore Sixth Form College is one mile from our Marlpit base, yet many Marlpit teenagers perceive it as inaccessible or unfamiliar. They have no prior physical contact with the campus or its staff before their first day.

Our evidence from running the pilot Second Chances course in early 2025 demonstrates that when structured, small-group work addresses these causes directly in Marlpit, young people complete their training and progress. Of the 24 young people who started that course, 19 finished and 11 secured an immediate next step. We are applying to remove the remaining transition barriers for this specific cohort.

## Q3. What will you do? The activities, the sequence, who delivers each part, where each part happens, and how many people take part. Show how the activities follow from the causes in Q2.

Over an 18-month grant period, we will run three sequential cohorts of a 12-week progression course based directly on our proven Second Chances model, delivering sustained casework to move 72 disengaged 16-to-19-year-olds in Marlpit into verified education, training, or employment.

Each 18-month cycle contains three distinct cohorts of 24 young people, structured across three delivery phases:

Phase 1: Identification, Triage, and Induction (Weeks 1–2 of each cohort)
- Activity: We identify 24 participants per cohort (72 total) directly from our three-evening-a-week Marlpit youth club, our supper club, and our family advice desk. Staff conduct one-to-one diagnostic interviews to assess past educational attainment, practical barriers (such as travel or equipment costs), and vocational interests.
- Location: The Marlpit delivery site.
- Delivered by: One full-time Lead Progression Worker (funded by this grant) supported by our regular volunteer team.

Phase 2: 12-Week Progression Course Delivery (Weeks 3–14 of each cohort)
- Activity: A 12-week, modular curriculum running two half-day sessions per week. Modules cover core workplace literacy, practical IT routines, CV preparation, interview rehearsal, and personal routine management. During weeks 6 and 10, the cohort undertakes guided visits to Dunmore Sixth Form College, meeting tutors and familiarising themselves with the campus layout, alongside visits to local vocational training and employer settings that we will identify and schedule across the grant period.
- Location: The Marlpit site for classroom modules; Dunmore Sixth Form College (one mile away) for site inductions and admissions meetings.
- Delivered by: The Lead Progression Worker and one part-time Project Worker (0.5 FTE, funded by this grant).

Phase 3: Transition Casework and Destination Handover (Weeks 15–20 of each cohort)
- Activity: Dedicated keywork to secure enrolment and course start dates. Staff provide accompanied registration visits, assist with bursary paperwork, and maintain weekly check-ins during the first four weeks of the participant's new placement.
- Location: Marlpit site, Dunmore Sixth Form College, and designated local destination sites.
- Delivered by: Lead Progression Worker.

Sequence across the 18-month grant:
- Months 1–6: Delivery of Cohort 1 (24 participants).
- Months 7–12: Delivery of Cohort 2 (24 participants).
- Months 13–18: Delivery of Cohort 3 (24 participants).

This sequence directly eliminates the causes identified in Q2: it draws isolated young people directly from open-access sessions, rebuilds core routines through small-group delivery in Marlpit, and provides continuous, accompanied transition casework into Dunmore Sixth Form College and local entry-level employers.

## Q4. What will change, and how will you know? Intended outcomes, the numbers you expect, how you will measure them, and what you would count as this work not having worked.

Across 18 months, we will achieve four clear, measurable outcomes for 72 young people aged 16 to 19 living in Marlpit:

1. Retention and Course Completion: At least 57 of the 72 starters (79%, matching our 2025 pilot completion rate of 19 out of 24) will complete the 12-week course, submit an updated CV, and complete an exit interview.
2. Direct Progression to Verified Next Steps: At least 39 of the 72 starters (54%, building on the 11 of 24 achieved in our pilot) will transition into a verified next step within two months of course completion. We target the following specific destinations:
   - 24 participants enrolled at Dunmore Sixth Form College across two academic intake cycles.
   - 9 participants moving into formal apprenticeships or vocational training with local providers we will engage.
   - 6 participants moving into contracted, paid employment.
3. Sustained Engagement at Destination: At least 30 of the 39 progressed individuals (77%) will remain enrolled in their course or employed at their 30-day destination milestone.
4. Independent Advice and Entitlement Take-up: All 72 participants will receive an income and bursary review through our advice desk, ensuring eligible learners secure 16–19 bursaries or travel support.

Measurement tools:
- Initial registration files recording baseline education and employment status.
- Weekly attendance registers for all classroom sessions in Marlpit.
- Formal enrolment confirmations, college letters, and student ID verification from Dunmore Sixth Form College.
- Written apprenticeship agreements or signed employment contracts collected at exit.
- 30-day post-progression follow-up calls and verification logs with admissions staff, employers, and participants.

What we would count as not having worked:
We will consider this project to have failed if fewer than 30 young people (under 42% of all 72 starters) progress into a verified college place, apprenticeship, or paid job, or if fewer than 18 young people enroll at Dunmore Sixth Form College. We will also count it as underperforming if our course drop-out rate exceeds 30% (fewer than 50 course completers). If month-six tracking for Cohort 1 shows progression conversion below 40%, we will halt intake, consult Dunmore Sixth Form College admissions staff, and restructure the practical curriculum before starting Cohort 2.

## Q5. What happens after the grant? Name what continues, who owns it, what it costs and who pays.

What continues:
The core practice that outlasts this grant is the permanent, embedded Progression Gateway operating out of our Marlpit youth facility. Specifically, the structured 12-week modular course materials, the intake diagnostic tools, and the formal referral protocol with Dunmore Sixth Form College established during this project will become permanent, standard operating procedures for Halewater Commons Trust. 

Who owns it:
Halewater Commons Trust owns the model, the intake materials, and the delivery methodology. Our permanent youth work team and the volunteer team that supports our weekly supper club and advice desk will own and run the referral routines.

What it costs to run without the grant:
Running the ongoing progression practice after Month 18 will cost £12,500 per year. Because the curriculum, delivery resources, college partnership agreements, and staff training will be fully developed and tested during the grant period, delivery will no longer require two dedicated expansion posts. Instead, the practice will be integrated into our core youth work rota, requiring 0.25 FTE of qualified youth worker coordination time (£8,500), venue operating costs in Marlpit (£2,500), and participant travel/resource bursaries (£1,500).

Who pays for it:
Halewater Commons Trust will absorb this £12,500 annual running cost directly into our core operating budget. Our organizational expenditure is £214,000 per year across our Marlpit operations. By demonstrating verified progression outcomes for 39 local young people to Dunmore Sixth Form College and regional stakeholders, we will allocate this £12,500 from our existing core community donations, trust funding streams, and unrestricted reserves that currently maintain our weekly advice desk and supper club. The Wren Hill Foundation grant will have funded the design, initial staffing, testing, and full institutional integration of a permanent progression route in Marlpit; once established, our ongoing core budget maintains it without requiring external grant renewals.
