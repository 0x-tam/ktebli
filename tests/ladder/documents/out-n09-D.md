## Q1. Who are you and what do you do?

Halewater Commons Trust is a charitable incorporated organisation, constituted in 2016, working on the east side of Kelverton, a post-industrial town of about 41,000 people in northern England. We have six paid staff (4.2 full-time equivalent), about 25 regular volunteers, and total expenditure of £214,000 in the last filed financial year, with two years of accounts filed. Registration with the charity regulator is current.

Three things run week in, week out. Open-access youth sessions for 11 to 19 year olds run three evenings a week in Marlpit, the ward on the east bank of the river, and since October 2024 from a second site on the Ferry Bank estate, north of the old goods yard. A weekly supper club uses St Aidan's Parish Hall in Marlpit, hired at £40 an evening, and served 46 sittings in the year to March 2025. A small advice desk supports families in the same two neighbourhoods.

The youth work is led by Priya Raval, our delivery coordinator since 2019, who holds a level 3 youth work qualification and personally runs four of the six weekly sessions in Marlpit. Young people reach us three ways: they walk in, their families use the advice desk, or a statutory service refers them. Barrowfield Youth Justice Team sent nine young people to us between April 2024 and March 2025, six of them living in Ferry Bank.

We already move young people on. Since 2023 we have referred school-leavers to Dunmore Sixth Form College, a mile from our Marlpit site; 27 were referred in 2024-25 and 18 enrolled. A twelve-week progression course, Second Chances, ran in Marlpit from January to April 2025: 24 started, 19 finished, and 11 went into a college place, an apprenticeship or paid work.

## Q2. What is the problem, and how do you know?

Young people in Marlpit and Ferry Bank leave school and stop, and nothing local picks them up. Our own numbers show the shape of it. Of 24 who started Second Chances, five never finished and eight finished without reaching a next step. Of 27 referred to Dunmore Sixth Form College, nine did not enrol. That gap between referral and enrolment is the problem we intend to act on.

The causes we can see from the room, not just the symptoms:

- Money and the census. In Marlpit, 38 per cent of households sat in the lowest income decile at the 2021 census. A college place a mile away still costs bus fares, kit and a lost part-time shift, and families make that calculation in September.
- The handover fails. A referral is a piece of paper. Nobody walks the young person to enrolment, sits through the interview, or finds out in week three that they have stopped attending. Our nine non-enrolments in 2024-25 fell into that space.
- Ferry Bank is further out and newer to us. Our second site opened there in October 2024 and 31 young people attended in its first term, which tells us about reach and nothing yet about progression. Six of the nine young people referred by Barrowfield Youth Justice Team live there. We have no progression course on that side of the river at all.
- Confidence and record. Young people who left school badly do not believe a course will take them, and several have a youth justice contact they expect to be asked about.

Why these two places and not the next one over: we run six weekly sessions in them, we hire two buildings in them, and every one of the 14 minibus trips we bought from Northgate Minibus Hire between April 2024 and March 2025 picked up in Marlpit. We do not have that standing anywhere else in Kelverton.

## Q3. What will you do?

We will run four progression cohorts of 16 young people aged 15 to 19, 64 in total, over 18 months from 1 March 2027 to 31 August 2028, with a named worker attached to each young person for six months after their cohort ends.

- Cohorts 1 and 3 run in Marlpit at St Aidan's Parish Hall, two evenings a week for twelve weeks, at £40 an evening. Cohorts 2 and 4 run on the Ferry Bank side of the river at Wharfside Boathouse, which we have hired for 18 days across the summers of 2024 and 2025 at £120 a day, so we know the building and the young people know how to get to it.
- Content follows the Second Chances design: what a next step actually involves, a practical block, an interview, and a visit. Priya Raval leads the curriculum and runs the Marlpit cohorts. A sessional youth worker, recruited in the first six weeks, runs Ferry Bank alongside her, with volunteers from our existing 25 supporting each session.
- Recruitment happens through our open-access sessions in both neighbourhoods, the family advice desk, and Barrowfield Youth Justice Team.
- Six trips per cohort, 24 in total, are bought from Northgate Minibus Hire at £95, picking up in Marlpit or Ferry Bank. These go to Dunmore Sixth Form College, to a further education provider and to local employers, so the destination is a place the young person has stood in before they apply to it.
- Weeks nine to twelve are the handover. Each young person leaves with one named next step, an application submitted, and a date. Priya Raval or the sessional worker attends the college interview or employer meeting with them.
- After the cohort, follow-through runs from a fortnightly evening drop-in at St Aidan's Parish Hall, 30 evenings across the grant, plus phone contact at weeks two, six, twelve and twenty-four of the next step.

## Q4. What will change, and how will you know?

Of 64 young people who start, we expect 51 to finish their twelve weeks and 32 to reach a defined next step within six months of finishing. Those figures come from Second Chances, where 19 of 24 finished and 11 progressed. We are holding the completion rate and the progression rate flat rather than assuming the handover work improves them, because Ferry Bank is untested for progression.

Of the 32, we expect 16 to enrol at Dunmore Sixth Form College. Our 2024-25 record was 18 enrolments from 27 referrals, so we plan 24 college referrals to get 16 in. The remaining 16 go to an apprenticeship, a paid job, a training provider place or a return to school sixth form. At least 12 of the 32 will live in Ferry Bank.

Measurement is a spreadsheet Priya Raval owns, one row per young person, with start date, sessions attended, the named next step, the provider, the enrolment or start date, and the four follow-up calls. Retention at weeks 12 and 24 of the next step is recorded as still there, moved to something else, or stopped. An independent evaluator interviews 15 young people and both delivery workers at months nine and eighteen.

This has not worked if fewer than 20 of the 64 reach a named next step, or if fewer than 20 of those who do are still in it at week twelve, or if Ferry Bank produces fewer than six progressions across its two cohorts. We will report those figures whichever way they fall.

## Q5. What happens after the grant?

Three things continue.

The twelve-week course becomes the standing shape of our youth work in both neighbourhoods rather than a funded add-on. Priya Raval owns it and holds the curriculum, session plans and referral scripts.

The handover practice continues at no extra cost: attending the interview, submitting the application in the room, and the four follow-up calls. That is a way of working, not a budget line.

The fortnightly follow-through drop-in continues at St Aidan's Parish Hall at £40 an evening, 26 evenings a year, £1,040, staffed by the coordinator and volunteers. Our trustees will meet that from unrestricted funds within a £214,000 turnover, and we will put it in the annual budget from year one of the grant so it is never a new decision.

Running one cohort a year without a grant costs about £3,400: 24 evenings of hall or boathouse hire, three minibus trips and participant costs, delivered on existing staff time. We will fund it from unrestricted income and the supper club's local support base, and seek grant funding only to run more than one cohort a year.
