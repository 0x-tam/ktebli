## Q1. Who are you and what do you do?

Halewater Commons Trust is a charitable incorporated organisation, constituted in 2016 and registered in the United Kingdom, based in Kelverton, a post-industrial town of about 41,000 people in northern England. We have six paid staff (4.2 FTE), around 25 regular volunteers, two years of accounts filed and expenditure of GBP 214,000 in the last filed year. We apply on our own behalf as the accountable body.

We run open-access youth sessions for 11 to 19 year olds, a weekly supper club and a small family advice desk on the east side of town. Youth sessions run three evenings a week in Marlpit, the ward on the east bank of the river, where 38 per cent of households sat in the lowest income decile at the 2021 census. In the year to March 2025 the supper club served 46 sittings at St Aidan's Parish Hall, the venue proposed for cohort delivery, and we ran 14 off-site trips by hired minibus, all picking up in Marlpit.

Youth work is led by Priya Raval, delivery coordinator since 2019, who holds a level 3 youth work qualification and runs four of the six weekly Marlpit sessions. Since 2023 we have referred school-leavers to Dunmore Sixth Form College, a mile from Marlpit. Marlpit is the only place proposed for this project.

## Q2. What is the problem, and how do you know?

Our own records for 2024-25 shape this application. We referred 27 young people to Dunmore Sixth Form College and 18 enrolled. Our twelve-week progression course, Second Chances, ran in Marlpit from January to April 2025: of 24 starters, 19 finished and 11 moved into a college place, apprenticeship or paid work.

Places were available and young people wanted them. The loss happens between a decision made in a youth session and settled attendance inside an institution: a gap in accompaniment, not in supply. We identify five causes:

1. Nobody walks a young person through application, interview, enrolment and induction, so intentions are not converted into places taken up.
2. Nothing is handed over. The college receives a referral but not the practical knowledge (travel, caring duties, literacy support, anxiety triggers) that would aid retention.
3. The first weeks of attendance are unsupported, so early absence goes unnoticed and becomes withdrawal.
4. Practical barriers concentrate in one ward: the cost and unfamiliarity of a mile-long journey across the river, no quiet place to complete an online application, no adult to attend an interview.
5. Our own progression support has been episodic. One twelve-week course is not a route a sixteen year old can join when they need it.

## Q3. What will you do?

**Marlpit Next Step**, 18 months, delivered at St Aidan's Parish Hall and the Marlpit venues where our youth sessions already run, with accompaniment to Dunmore Sixth Form College one mile away.

A **progression keyworker** (new post, 0.8 FTE) runs three repeating twelve-week cohorts of 22 young people, 66 participants in total, staying with each through application, interview, enrolment and the first six weeks of attendance. Priya Raval supervises at 0.1 FTE and holds the college relationship, capped so it does not displace her session delivery.

| Months | What happens | Where and who |
|---|---|---|
| 1-2 | Negotiate and sign a bilateral referral and handover agreement with the college: referral route, handover contents, consent wording, named contact for six-week confirmation; keyworker recruitment | Coordinator, college |
| 2-3, 7-8, 12-13 | Cohort recruitment from youth sessions, supper club, advice desk and school or college referrals; readiness conversation per young person | Marlpit venues |
| 3-6, 8-11, 13-16 | Cohort delivery: application and interview practice, course and apprenticeship options, travel rehearsal, literacy and confidence work, college and employer visits by hired minibus | St Aidan's Hall |

| 7-17 | Up to six volunteer mentors recruited from existing volunteers, trained, shadowing cohorts 2 and 3 | Coordinator |
| 14-18 | Curriculum, handover protocol and mentor handbook written up and reviewed with participants and college | Coordinator |

## Q4. What will change, and how will you know?

We count the step, not the sentiment. Targets across three cohorts:

| Indicator | Target | Baseline |
|---|---|---|
| Cohort starts | 66 | 24 (Second Chances 2025) |
| Completions | 52 | 19 (2025) |
| Confirmed offers (college, apprenticeship, training, job) | 46 | 11 progressions (2025) |
| Enrolments or job starts | 40 | 18 of 27 referred (2024-25) |
| Still attending or in post at six weeks | 34 | not previously tracked |
| Written handovers to the college | 40 | not previously done |
| Volunteer mentors trained and active | 6 | 0 |

Evidence comes from auditable records we already keep: a participant register with attendance per session; a signed offer or confirmation letter for each offer counted; written confirmation of enrolment and six-week attendance from the college's named contact where the young person consents; a dated handover document per young person; and a case note per accompaniment episode. No indicator depends on a survey being returned.

We report at months 6, 12 and 18, giving numbers against target, recorded reasons for every young person who did not reach the next step, and what we changed. Cohort 1's shortfalls redesign cohort 2 recruitment, and cohort 2's redesign cohort 3; the month 18 report states which of the five causes in Q2 the project did and did not move.

Two risks are named openly. The college relationship carries the six-week measure; if the agreement is not concluded, the contact changes or consent is withheld, we fall back on the young person's confirmation plus a payslip, enrolment letter or timetable, and say so. One post carries delivery; the 0.1 FTE supervision doubles as absence cover, and mentors are drawn from our existing supervised volunteers.

## Q5. What happens after the grant?

Three things outlast the grant. The referral and handover arrangement with the college, set down in writing in the first six months, is an organisational agreement rather than a personal favour, so it survives staff change on either side. Up to six trained mentors remain within our existing volunteer body. The curriculum and mentor handbook are written up in months 14 to 18 so the post can be restarted from a document rather than from memory.

We do not claim that 0.8 FTE keyworking will continue unfunded. Months 12 to 18 are used to secure continuation: the college arrangement and three progression reports give us evidence for statutory and larger trust funders, and the written curriculum makes the post transferable to a host organisation if we cannot fund it ourselves.
