## Q1. Who are you and what do you do?

Halewater Commons Trust is a charitable incorporated organisation, constituted in 2016, working on the east side of Kelverton, a post-industrial town of about 41,000 people in northern England. We have six paid staff (4.2 full-time equivalent) and around 25 regular volunteers. Total expenditure in our last filed financial year was £214,000, and two years of accounts are filed.

Three things run week in, week out. Open-access youth sessions for 11 to 19 year olds run three evenings a week in Marlpit, the ward on the east bank of the river. A supper club runs weekly. A small advice desk supports families.

Since 2023 we have referred school-leavers to Dunmore Sixth Form College, a mile from our Marlpit site. In the 2024-25 academic year we referred 27 young people and 18 enrolled. From January to April 2025 we ran a twelve-week progression course in Marlpit called Second Chances: 24 young people started, 19 finished, and 11 moved into a college place, an apprenticeship or paid work.

## Q2. What is the problem, and how do you know?

Young people in Marlpit leave school and stall. Our own numbers show the shape of it. Of 24 who started Second Chances, 13 did not reach a college place, an apprenticeship or paid work. Of 27 referred to Dunmore Sixth Form College, nine did not enrol. These are not people who refused help; they are people who came to us and still did not land.

Marlpit is where our youth sessions run three evenings a week, and it is the poorest ward we work in: 38 per cent of households sat in the lowest income decile at the 2021 census. The river is the dividing line. Almost everything that decides a young person's next twelve months, including the college, sits off the estate, and a mile is a long way when nobody in the house has done that walk.

We intend to act on three causes we can see in our own delivery records rather than on the symptom of low enrolment.

First, the gap between finishing and starting. Second Chances ended in April 2025. College enrolment happens in late summer. Nobody held those young people through the months in between, and that is where the 13 went.

Second, unaccompanied first contact. Our referral conversion is 18 in 27. The nine who did not enrol were sent rather than taken.

Third, a thin practical floor. Our advice desk sees the same blockers in families: no identity documents, no bus fare, no interview clothes, nobody at home who has filled in an enrolment form.

We are honest that this evidence is our own casework and two cohorts of delivery data, not a study. Part of what we will build with this grant is a tracked record good enough to argue from.

## Q3. What will you do?

We will run Second Chances three times over 15 months, from April 2027 to June 2028, for 16 young people each time. That is 48 participants aged 16 to 19, recruited from our own Marlpit youth sessions and from our advice desk.

Each cohort follows the same twelve-week sequence at our Marlpit site, then a defined follow-through period.

- Weeks 1 to 4: two sessions a week, delivered by our youth staff, on the practical floor. Identity documents, bank accounts, bus routes, enrolment forms. The advice desk handles document and benefit problems in the same building on the same day.
- Weeks 5 to 8: accredited employability units delivered by a sessional tutor we will contract, plus one visit off the estate each week, staff-accompanied, to the college and to local employers.
- Weeks 9 to 12: one named destination per young person, written down, with the application actually submitted before the course ends.
- Follow-through, weeks 13 to 26: a progression worker, 0.6 full-time equivalent, keeps contact fortnightly and walks each young person into the door of their destination on the first day.

The progression worker is the change from 2025. This post exists to cover the gap between finishing and starting, and it is the single biggest thing we are asking you to pay for.

Cohorts start April 2027, September 2027 and February 2028, timed so that cohort one reaches college enrolment in the late summer window and the others reach apprenticeship and employer recruitment points. Our volunteers support sessions and accompany visits, with a short training block for around ten of them in the first two months.

## Q4. What will change, and how will you know?

Our outcome is a young person standing in a named place they were not going to reach.

Of 48 starters, we expect:

- 38 to complete twelve weeks. Second Chances completion was 19 of 24, so this holds our own rate.
- 30 to have a named destination and a submitted application by week 12.
- 24 to be enrolled at Dunmore Sixth Form College, started on an apprenticeship, or in paid work of 16 hours a week or more, and still there at week 26.

The 24 is a deliberate step up from 11 of 24 in 2025, and we are basing it on two things: the follow-through post, and our referral record of 18 enrolments from 27 referrals, which shows what happens when contact is maintained to the door.

Measurement is a single spreadsheet held by the progression worker, one row per young person, updated at week 12, week 20 and week 26. We record the destination by name, the provider, the start date, and whether the young person is still there. Verification is the enrolment or contract confirmation the young person shows us, or a call to the provider with their consent. We will report cohort by cohort rather than in one total at the end.

We would count this as not having worked if fewer than 18 of the 48 are in a named destination at week 26, or if any cohort drops below five. Eighteen is roughly the rate we already achieve without a follow-through post; below that, the post has bought nothing and we would say so and stop.

## Q5. What happens after the grant?

Two things continue.

The follow-through practice continues. Walking a young person to the door on day one, and fortnightly contact for three months after a course ends, becomes standard in our youth work rather than a funded add-on. Our youth staff own it. It costs staff time we already have, roughly four hours a week absorbed into the existing three-evening rota, plus about £600 a year in bus fares, which our trustees will hold in the annual budget.

The tracking record continues. One row per young person, destination named and verified at three points, owned by our senior youth worker. It costs nothing to run and it is what we will use to argue for the next round of funding, whether from a local authority commissioner, a college partnership, or another trust.

We are not claiming the twelve-week course itself continues at three cohorts a year without funding. It does not. What we will have at the end is 15 months of verified destination data on 48 young people in one ward, which is the case we need to make for it.
