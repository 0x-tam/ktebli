## Q1. Who are you and what do you do?

Halewater Commons Trust is a charitable incorporated organisation, constituted in 2016, working in Kelverton, a post-industrial town of about 41,000 people in northern England. Our work is on the east side of town, in Marlpit.

We run three things week in, week out. Open-access youth sessions for 11 to 19 year olds. A weekly supper club. A small advice desk for families. The same families use all three, which is the point of running them together: a parent who comes to the advice desk about a letter from a creditor has a fifteen year old in the youth session on a Tuesday night, and we know both of them.

We have six paid staff, 4.2 full-time equivalent, and around 25 regular volunteers. Total expenditure in our last filed financial year was £214,000, and we have two years of accounts filed. One of the people involved in our work is Priya Raval.

We have run a programme called Second Chances. We have a relationship with Dunmore Sixth Form College. We use St Aidan's Parish Hall as a venue, and we hire minibuses from Northgate Minibus Hire when we need to move a group. We should be straightforward with you: our records of past cohort sizes and destinations are not quantified in a form we can put in front of an assessor, so we are not going to quote numbers we cannot stand behind. What we can show is that we have been open in the same neighbourhood since 2016 and that the young people keep coming back.

## Q2. What is the problem, and how do you know?

Young people in Marlpit reach 16, leave school, and stop. They do not enrol anywhere, or they enrol and are gone by December. We see them again at the Tuesday youth session at 17, out of education, out of work, and no longer anybody's responsibility.

Our evidence is our own contact, not a study. The 11 to 19 sessions, the supper club and the advice desk give us the same households from three directions, and we watch the same pattern in each of them.

The causes we intend to act on are the ones we can actually reach:

- Nobody walks the last hundred yards with them. There is a difference between being told about a college and being taken to it, enrolled, and checked on in week six. Enrolment paperwork, bursary forms and a first day among strangers are where our young people fall out.
- English and maths sit under everything. A young person who cannot get the grade cannot get onto the course, and by 16 they have decided that is settled.
- Getting to Dunmore Sixth Form College is a real distance from the east side of town, and cost and unfamiliarity make it feel further than it is.
- Households under pressure cannot absorb a false start. When a parent is at our advice desk about arrears, a college application slides.

We are not claiming to fix Marlpit's economy. We are claiming that the drop-out happens at identifiable moments, that we are in the room when it happens, and that a person whose job it is to hold on through those moments changes the result.

## Q3. What will you do?

We will run a 15-month progression programme, from 1 April 2027 to 30 June 2028, for 42 young people aged 15 to 19 from Marlpit, in three cohorts of 14. We are calling it Second Chances, continuing the name we have used before.

Recruitment is from our own rooms. Cohort 1 is recruited April to May 2027 from the Tuesday and Thursday youth sessions, the supper club and referrals from the advice desk. Cohorts 2 and 3 start in October 2027 and February 2028.

Each cohort runs for 20 weeks in St Aidan's Parish Hall:

- Weeks 1 to 4: one-to-one progression planning with a progression worker we will recruit into this grant, 0.6 FTE. Each young person ends with a written plan naming one destination and one fallback.
- Weeks 1 to 20: a weekly evening group session at St Aidan's, delivered by the progression worker with two of our youth workers and volunteer mentors drawn from our 25 regular volunteers. Twelve mentors will be recruited and trained across the grant.
- Weeks 3 to 16: functional skills English and maths, two hours a week, delivered by a sessional tutor we will contract. This runs at St Aidan's alongside the group session.
- Weeks 6 to 12: visits out. Northgate Minibus Hire takes each cohort to Dunmore Sixth Form College twice, so the building stops being abstract, plus two visits to local employers or training providers we will approach in the first two months.
- Weeks 13 to 20: applications, bursary forms, interview practice, and the enrolment appointment itself, with the progression worker going with them.
- Weeks 21 to 32: aftercare. Fortnightly contact, and a check at week six of the destination and again at week twelve.

Priya Raval leads on the relationship with Dunmore Sixth Form College and on referral and aftercare arrangements with them. Our advice desk takes the household side, so a benefit or arrears problem does not end a place.

## Q4. What will change, and how will you know?

Of the 42 young people, we expect 30 to complete their cohort and 26 to start a named next step within twelve weeks of finishing. Of those 26, we expect 16 to enrol at Dunmore Sixth Form College and 10 to start a named apprenticeship, employment with training, or a training provider course. We expect 20 of the 26 still to be there at the twelve-week check.

These targets are design decisions. We have no conversion rate from previous cohorts to extrapolate from, and we are not going to pretend otherwise. They are set at a level a 0.6 FTE worker holding 14 young people at a time can realistically deliver, and we will report against them honestly whichever way they go.

Measurement is a single spreadsheet, updated weekly by the progression worker: attendance, plan agreed, application submitted, enrolment confirmed, week-six check, week-twelve check. Confirmation comes from the destination, not from the young person's say-so. We will also record functional skills entries and passes, and household issues resolved through the advice desk.

This will not have worked if fewer than 15 of the 42 start a named next step, or if fewer than 12 are still in it at twelve weeks. At that point the model is wrong, not under-resourced, and we will say so in our final report rather than ask you for more.

## Q5. What happens after the grant?

Three things continue.

The progression conversation stays inside the Tuesday and Thursday youth sessions. Two of our existing youth workers will be trained during the grant to do progression planning and enrolment support, so it becomes part of core youth work rather than a separate project. That costs staff time we already fund.

The volunteer mentor group continues. Twelve trained mentors, recruited and inducted during the grant, held by our existing volunteer arrangements. Ongoing cost is training refreshers and expenses, roughly £1,500 a year, met from our general funds.

The referral and aftercare route to Dunmore Sixth Form College continues, owned by Priya Raval and written down as a short agreement during the grant so it does not depend on one relationship.

Running the light-touch version without a dedicated worker costs us about £4,000 a year: mentor costs, room hire at St Aidan's Parish Hall, and occasional minibus hire from Northgate Minibus Hire. Our trustees will carry that in the annual budget against expenditure of the order of £214,000. If the outcome numbers hold, we will use them to seek funding for the 0.6 FTE post again, but the practice does not stop if we fail to.
