We understand that AI tools can help charities save time on writing applications and we
support charities using these technologies. However, we want to help charities use this

2. Background
When someone’s in a home that’s a good place to live, they have more than a roof over
their heads - they have safety and security they can afford. They have enough space for
the people and things they love. A place that’s comfortable, that they can be proud of.
That they can welcome a friend for tea. The choice to stick glow-in-the-dark stars to the
ceiling of their children’s bedroom. They walk through their front door and their
shoulders relax.
For many people, a safe and secure home is not a reality. Homelessness is increasing,
yet the evidence shows that it can often be prevented before it happens. This is
especially true for people going through certain key moments of change when the risk
of becoming homeless can be significantly higher.
Homelessness has many causes, which means prevention is complex and requires
different approaches at different stages. This fund takes the five stages of




Page 2
homelessness prevention as its starting point1, reflected in the five pillars of prevention2
shown below.
The fund is focused on supporting organisations that deliver targeted homelessness
prevention 3. Within the Welsh Government’s Homelessness Strategy and Action
Plans to End Homelessness, this is described as Secondary Prevention.




We believe local community organisations have a unique role to play in preventing
homelessness. At the key moments when people are most at risk, they are often the
first to recognise the challenges someone is facing and the best placed to offer timely
support. Their deep understanding of local communities, combined with trusted
relationships, and practical expertise, means they can reach people early and help
prevent homelessness before it happens.

3. Programme Overview
We know that there are key points in people’s lives when the risk of homelessness
increases significantly. These moments of change can create uncertainty and
instability, particularly when people do not have the right support around them.
We also know that community organisations play a vital role in preventing
homelessness. By building trusted relationships, recognising risk early and providing
targeted support, they can help people navigate challenging life moments and avoid
reaching crisis point.
This programme will support organisations whose main purpose is to work with people
who are at one of the following turning points:
leaving prison - People leaving prison face a much higher risk of homelessness than
the wider population. In England, they experience homelessness at rates around 40
times higher than the general population. In Wales,11% of households found to be
eligible for a homelessness relief duty reported a prison as their last settled
accommodation4.
leaving care - (up to age 25). Young people leaving care are at increased risk of
homelessness as they move into adulthood. Research shows 33% of care leavers
become homeless in the first two years after leaving care, while 25% of all single
homeless people have been in care at some point in their lives5. In England, the number
of young care leavers aged 18–20 approaching their local authority as homeless has
increased by 54% over the last five years6.
leaving an abusive relationship - People leaving abusive relationships often face
difficult choices about their safety, housing and financial security. Recent research
suggests that domestic abuse accounts for 25 - 33% of homelessness presentations to
local authorities across England, Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland7.
leaving the asylum system on securing leave to remain in the UK - People granted
refugee status through the asylum system in the UK can face a heightened risk
homelessness during the move-on period. Once leave to remain is granted, support
arrangements can change quickly, while access to suitable housing remains limited.

4. Who this programme is for
Your organisation’s primary purpose and activity should be to support people through
one of these life turning points. You will be working alongside people over the longer
term, helping them reduce their risk of homelessness, overcome barriers, and move
forward with greater stability, confidence, and opportunity to build the future they want
for themselves.

5. Number of grants available
We will award at least 40 grants across England and Wales in this funding round. This
programme offers unrestricted grants over four years. This means you can use the
funding in the way that best supports your organisation’s purpose and people you work
alongside. This includes core costs, staffing, organisational development and building
reserves. There are two grant sizes available:
    •    £100,000 paid over four years through annual payments of £25,000
    •    £200,000 paid over four years through annual payments of £50,000
Which grant can you apply for?
    •    If your organisation’s most recent annual income was less than £100,000, you
         can apply for a £100,000 grant.
    •    If your organisation’s most recent annual income was between £100,000 and £2
         Million, you can apply for either a £100,000 grant or a £200,000.
To determine eligibility, we will use the annual income shown in your organisation’s
most recent set of accounts filed with the Charity Commission and/or Companies
House, as applicable.
As an Open and Trusting funder, we are committed to being transparent and helping
organisations decide whether this opportunity is right for them before investing time in
submitting an application.

Question 3: What do you do and how you work?
   a) What you do:
Your organisation’s primary purpose must be the direct delivery of targeted support for
people experiencing one or more of the life turning points where the risk of
homelessness is higher:
   •     People who are leaving prison or young offenders’ institutions
   •     Young people aged up to 25 who are leaving or who have left care to live
         independently
   •     People leaving an abusive relationship
   •     People leaving the asylum system on securing leave to remain, including
         unaccompanied asylum-seeking children
Whilst your work will help to prevent homelessness, it will not be solely focussed on
housing support.
We expect organisations to provide holistic, person-centred support that helps people
navigate one of the life turning points identified within this programme. This may
include addressing immediate needs such as immigration, welfare or health-related
issues. However, any immediate support should form part of a broader approach that
builds longer-term relationships and helps people develop greater stability, wellbeing,
connections, and opportunities, where they can be empowered to have control over
their lives. Where possible it should also support people before and after the critical life
moment, for example supporting prisoners ‘through the gate’ or asylum seekers when
applying for and then securing leave to remain.
Alongside this, you will support people to access and sustain safe, suitable housing.
You may do this directly through the management of social purpose housing and/ or by
working closely with partners who do.

b) How you deliver your services:
We will look at how your organisation delivers its support, as well as what support or

following criteria:
Long-term, relationship support (In-depth services):
Your organisation will support people over the long term, building trusted relationships
that help them navigate one of the life turning points identified within this programme.
 It is likely that you provide a range of support tailored to individual circumstances and
needs. When you refer someone to another service, you remain involved and continue
to support them and track their progress, helping to ensure they receive the support
they need.
Person-centred support and personal plans:
We will support organisations that place people at the centre of their work. This means

You will work with the people you support to understand their goals, identify the support
they need, and agree together how you will help them achieve greater stability,
wellbeing and opportunity. We expect funded organisations to provide some individual
1:1 support. Organisations that provide only group-based activities or support, are

through this programme.

Question 7: How does your organisation approach equity, diversity and inclusion?

We know that people’s experiences and opportunities are not the same. Some people
face additional, barriers, discrimination, or disadvantage because of how they are
perceived or treated by others. This may relate to characteristics such as race or
ethnicity, age, disability, gender identity, religion or belief, sex, and sexual orientation. It
can also be shaped by poverty, social class or living in a rural or isolated community
We believe organisations need to understand these differences and respond to them,
ensuring that people can access support in ways that work for them.
In your response, we would like to understand how your organisation:
   •      understands the communities you serve and the different experiences,
          backgrounds and identities of the people within them.
   •      recognises where people face barriers, disadvantages, or inequity.

 EXAMPLE 1: Leaving Prison and / or Youth Offender Institutions
 An organisation based in North East England supports people leaving prison to
 secure employment quickly, helping to reduce the risk of homelessness at a critical
 transition point. By working with individuals both before and after release, the
 organisation provides a direct pathway into paid work, enabling people to build
 stability, independence and a sustainable future.
 Their approach is employer-focused, partnering with businesses to create accessible
 job opportunities while offering support to both the individual and the employer. This
 has resulted in strong outcomes, including high job retention rates and a significant
 reduction in reoffending and a compelling example of how targeted employment
 support can play a key role in homelessness prevention. 9In regions such as the North
 East, where reoffending rates are among the highest in the country10, their model is
 helping to achieve consistently better outcomes.



 EXAMPLE 2: Leaving care
 An organisation in Wales supports young people in care as they transition to
 independent living and adulthood. These young people are particularly vulnerable at
 a critical stage, often lacking essential skills and support networks. Many experience
 loneliness, isolation and difficulties managing independently after leaving
 placements.
 Support is provided for as long as needed, focusing on emotional, physical and
 mental wellbeing, alongside opportunities for personal growth. Around 250 young
 people a year are supported to strengthen their identity and reach their potential.

 EXAMPLE 3: Leaving an abusive relationship
 An organisation based in South West England helps women who have experienced
 domestic abuse and sexual violence. They have two refuges, where they
 accommodate up to 11 women and 16 children. Each family has their own fully
 equipped room, with a kitchen area. When women arrive at refuge they are allocated
 a worker, who supports them with practical and emotional issues whilst living in
 refuge. Women with children are also allocated a family worker.
 The charity has an IDVA (Independent Domestic Abuse Advocate) service, provides
 relocation support and provides a resettlement service including housing, budgeting,
 legal, employment, education and changes in schools’ provision. Through their
 Community Service they offer outreach to visit women in their new homes as well as
 support from their helpline and wellbeing programme.
 Their person-centred approach means support is tailored to each individual, with a
 strong focus on safety planning, emotional recovery and long-term stability. They also
 work with children and young people to address the impact of trauma early,
 alongside preventative work in schools and communities to break cycles of abuse.
 With deep roots in local communities and strong partnership links across statutory
 and voluntary services, they are a trusted and accessible provider. Their work
 demonstrates how targeted, trauma-informed support can reduce risk, prevent
 homelessness and enable survivors to move forward safely and independently.

 EXAMPLE 4: Leaving the asylum system on securing the right to remain
 Tight deadlines to “move on” post decision rarely provides enough time to secure
 income and permanent housing for asylum seeks who have been granted leave to
 remain.
 An organisation based in North West England helps people in hotels plan for life after
 their asylum decision. This includes support with housing, welfare benefits,
 education, employment and other practical issues that become urgent once a
 decision is made.
 By working with people while they are still in hotels, the team aim to reduce the risk of
 homelessness and destitution once refugee status is granted. As well as securing

   1) What do you mean by your “main purpose”?
The main purpose of your organisation will be directly delivering targeted services for
people at one of the following turning points where the risk of future homelessness is
higher:
   •      People who are leaving prison or young offenders’ institutions
   •      Young people aged up to 25 who are leaving or who have left care to live
          independently
   •      People leaving an abusive relationship
   •      People leaving the asylum system on securing leave to remain, including
          unaccompanied asylum-seeking children
By main purpose, we mean most of your organisation’s resources (staff, volunteers,
projects and activities) should be directed at people going through one of these life
turning points.
Your focus will be working with people in criminal justice, with care leavers, victims and
survivors of domestic abuse, or with refugees and asylum seekers. The work may be
with children and young people, adults, or various ages e.g. whole families.
If your work with people at these specific life turning points is incidental, a small part of
your overall work, or not targeted, then you are unlikely to be funded.
We will use the information you provide on your application form, as well as publicly
available information on your website / social media or with the Charities Commission /
Companies House to understand what your main purpose is.


   2) Can we use AI to help write our application?

Yes.

We support charities using AI, including to help draft funding applications. We
understand the time and resource pressures many organisations face and recognise
that AI can be a useful tool. Used well, it can help reduce administrative burdens and
give organisations more time to focus on their core purpose.




Page 23
We read every application we receive and do not use AI to make funding decisions.
What matters most to us is understanding your organisation, the people you support
and the difference you make.

We have noticed an increasing number of applications that rely heavily on AI-generated
content. While this can save time, it can sometimes make applications feel generic and
make it harder for us to understand what is distinctive about an organisation. The
strongest applications help us hear your voice, understand your experience and see
what makes your work unique.

If you choose to use AI, we encourage you to use it as a tool to support the application
process rather than write the application for you. For example, it can be helpful for
generating first drafts, improving structure, checking grammar or reducing word counts.
However, we recommend reviewing and tailoring any AI-generated content, so it
accurately reflects your organisation and your work.

Things to consider when using AI

Keep your organisation's voice

We want to understand your organisation, your approach and what makes your work
your application. You remain responsible for ensuring that all information in your
application is accurate and up to date.

Protect sensitive information

Many AI tools retain or process information entered by users. Before sharing
information with an AI platform, make sure you understand how your data will be stored
and used.

As a rule, avoid sharing confidential, personal or sensitive information with free AI tools.
If you use a paid-for service, we recommend checking its data protection, privacy and
data retention policies to ensure your information is secure.

Our advice

Use AI to save time, not to replace your expertise. The people who know your
organisation best are the people within it. The strongest applications combine the
efficiency of AI with your own knowledge, experience and voice

distinctive. Applications that rely heavily on generic AI-generated content can make this
more difficult.

Specific examples, case studies and reflections from your own experience help bring
your work to life and give us a much clearer picture of the difference you make.

Check for accuracy

AI tools can sometimes generate information that is inaccurate, misleading or entirely
incorrect.

   4) Do you fund faith-based organisations?

Yes. We fund many faith-based organisations and recognise the important role they
play in supporting people and communities.




Page 25
However, we do not fund organisations where participation in faith-based activities
forms part of the services provided, unless the organisation exists specifically to
support people who share that faith.

Where an organisation is open to people of all faiths and none, we expect its services to

an organisation that excludes people from accessing services on the basis of a
protected characteristic, including race, religion, sex, sexual orientation, disability or
gender reassignment.

and inclusion and how these principles are reflected in your work.

We also do not fund organisations that require staff or volunteers to belong to a
particular faith unless this can be clearly justified as a genuine occupational
requirement, or the organisation exists specifically to serve people of that same faith.

   6) We had an unusual one-off legacy payment which is reported in our last set
      of accounts, which takes our income over £2m. As this was a one-off, can we
      be considered eligible?
No. We only fund organisations with an annual income of between £25,000 and £2
million in their last published accounts.


   7) We have a commitment to equity, diversity, and inclusion, but know that we
      could do better in this. Will this mean that we won’t be funded?
No. We will partner with charities that can demonstrate that they are committed to
ensuring that everyone who could benefit from its services can access them. However,
where an organisation understands and recognises that it could have a stronger policy
or practice, and demonstrates a commitment to improving in this, we may still consider
awarding a grant and would provide development support to enable them to strengthen
their practice.


   8) We have seven trustees and three senior managers. Four of the trustees are
      related. Are we eligible?
No. Given that the trustees are the most accountable people in the organisation, we
would consider that if most of the Trustees are related, the organisation would not be
eligible to apply.


   9) Our Chair and CEO are related. However, we have seven unrelated trustees
      and two senior managers. Are we eligible to apply?
Yes. However, at the assessment, we will review the conflicts of interest and loyalty
policy, how this works in practice, and how conflicts are managed.
