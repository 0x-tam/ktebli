# Ktebli — Business Blueprint v2

**One line:** A Lebanese organisation pastes the grant it is going for, answers a few questions, and gets a submission-ready proposal package in its own voice, with a hard guarantee that nobody else applying to that same grant can be given anything like it.

*Name: **Ktebli** (اكتبلي), Lebanese dialect for "write it for me", which is literally the customer's request. The wordmark is a stamp reading KTEBLI! because the name is an imperative and the exclamation belongs to it. Domains: **ktebli.com and ktebli.ai both verified available at registry level on 19 August 2026 — buy both immediately**, along with the @ktebli Instagram handle. The playful name is deliberately balanced by sober, competent design and copy; the name makes them smile, the page makes them trust. Palette is cream, grass green, coral and ink.*

*Runner-up, kept on file: Maktub AI (maktubai.com, also verified available) — "it is written" in Arabic, a triple pun on written proposals, AI writing, and destiny. If Ktebli ever proves too casual for institutional buyers, this is the fallback.*

## Language rules for anything a customer sees

Insider vocabulary is how you lose the exact person you are selling to, who writes a proposal twice a year on top of a real job. Three words have already been caught and banned in testing, and the pattern is worth keeping.

Never write **call**, **solicitation**, **NOFO** or **RFP**. Write "the grant you are going for" or "the donor's guidelines".

Never write **room**. The metaphor implies a space with limited capacity, so the first thing anyone asks is whether it can be full. Say plainly: we check your proposal against every other proposal we have written for that same grant.

Never write **red team** or **rubric**. Both are jargon that make a simple thing sound complicated. "Rubric" is just "how the donor will mark you". Keep the internal names in code and in this document, and keep them off the website.

Never write **mock**, **practice**, **simulated** or **dry run**. They make a real service sound like pretend, and the customer is paying real money for it. The customer-facing name is **the review**, described as **the deeper final review**: the whole package cross-checked against the grant's stated requirements, questions and published criteria, facts and figures verified for consistency, and the writing read closely — presented as part of producing the final document, never as a grading exercise.

Four rules added on 21 August 2026, after a full positioning pass:

**No numerical quality scores anywhere a customer can see.** Not "68/100", not "scored 90", not before-and-after numbers. Internal panel scores still drive pipeline decisions, but a number shown to a customer either overclaims (we cannot predict the donor's score) or underclaims (a first draft "scoring 72" says our work needed fixing). Where a donor publishes a rubric with point values we may use it internally to verify coverage, and we may say the proposal was "checked for alignment with the donor's published criteria" — never that we know what the donor would score it.

**No self-correction language.** Nothing that implies the first output was not our best work: no "weak sections strengthened", "re-scored", "improved draft", "extra quality passes", "rewritten until it passes". Safeguard loops exist internally; the customer-facing story is one continuous act of producing the strongest document. Stage labels, held-state notes, review reports and emails all follow this. The one visible exception stays deliberate: lock four openly says a too-similar draft is automatically rewritten, because there distinctiveness enforcement is the selling point.

**No geographic framing.** The product reads as for anyone, anywhere: no Lebanon, Beirut, or any named market, no "Made in Lebanon" badge, no named donors like the U.S. Embassy or EED on the site, no location-flavoured examples or placeholders, no ministry names in form hints. Later the same day the Arabic script came out of the footer too (now "Ktebli: Proposals like no other, ever."), the hero became "Get the proposal only you could have written", and the whole "About the review" paragraph under pricing was removed rather than rewritten — the Full tier's own bullet list carries that message now. This is site copy only: the go-to-market focus in this document is strategy, not copy, and stays.

**Time expectation is 5–30 minutes, depending on complexity.** Never "a couple of minutes" (makes the work feel trivial) and never a promise of an exact time. The line appears on the how-it-works step, an FAQ of its own, the checkout note, the tracking email and the order page.

The general test: if a programmes director reading on a phone would have to stop and work out what a word means, it is the wrong word.

## 1. Why this can work

The AI grant-tool market already exists and is priced as monthly software. Instrumentl runs $499 a month, Grantable $50 to $150, GrantBoost around $45 per module. All of them are built for an American nonprofit with a full-time development officer, and all of them draw the same two complaints in reviews: the writing is generic, and none of them know anything about the competition for the grant in question.

Those two complaints are your whole business.

The first gives you pricing shape. A Lebanese association applies three or four times a year. Asking it to carry a subscription for a tool it opens four times is a bad trade and it will not convert from an Instagram ad. Charging per proposal matches how the customer experiences the problem: there is a deadline, there is money on the table, there is a fee. Nobody is selling that way to this customer.

The second gives you the moat, and it is the thing you identified yourself. Lebanon has roughly 11,676 registered associations, of which the serious international-grant seekers are a small fraction, and the pool applying to any one grant is smaller still. The same three or four people at the Embassy, the EU Delegation or EED read every submission. If two of your customers send in proposals that rhyme, both are binned and your name in Beirut is finished inside a month. Nobody else in this category has to solve that, because their customers are spread across fifty American states. You have to, because your market is one small country. Solve it properly and the thing that could kill you becomes the thing you advertise.

## 2. Who you are selling to

A registered Lebanese association with three to fifteen staff and no dedicated grant writer. Someone there, usually the director or a programmes lead, writes the applications at night. They are going to the U.S. Embassy, the EU Delegation, EED, UNDP, GIZ, the British Embassy, Drosos, or a private foundation. They have written proposals before, some funded, and those documents are sitting in a folder doing nothing, which is exactly the asset the product needs.

Second, later and without chasing it, the freelance grant consultant in Beirut who charges $800 to $2,000 a proposal and will happily pay $299 for the first eighty percent. They will find you if the product is good.

Third, much later, the same product pointed at Jordan, Iraq and Egypt, where the donors and the conventions are nearly identical. Do not touch this until Lebanon works.

Donor forms were dropped deliberately. Every funder's forms differ, several are legally binding declarations signed by the legal representative, and completing one wrongly on a customer's behalf is a liability you do not want on a $449 product. Help them understand what a form is asking, do not sign it for them.

**The split between the tiers.** An earlier version put almost everything into $299, which left $449 with nothing to sell. The line now sits in a defensible place. $299 buys the complete written application: the narrative, the concept note, the budget and its justification, and the covering email, all in the organisation's own voice. $449 buys judgement on top of documents. Everything above the line is a thing you write; everything below it is somebody assessing what you wrote and telling you how to make it score higher.

Scoring lives only at $449, and that is the single most important pricing decision here. It is the one thing competitors cannot copy in a weekend, it is the thing that most changed the DAWRUNA outcome, and putting a cheap version of it in the middle tier would have made the top tier pointless. The workplan, the logframe, the risk table and the one-page summary moved up with it because they are the documents a serious applicant needs, and a serious applicant is buying $449 anyway.

$149 is the entry point and has to stay something a programmes officer can approve without a board conversation. $299 is where you want almost everybody, hence the badge.

**How to talk about the review.** Never describe it as finding faults, weaknesses or where the proposal fails. That framing tells the customer your own writing is defective, in the tier that costs the most, which is the opposite of what they are paying for. Frame it as gaining marks, not losing them: a panel reads it, scores it, and shows you where the next marks are and how to take them. Same mechanism, and it sounds like a coach rather than an apology.

The honest and more impressive version of the DAWRUNA story is that the panel ran at design stage, scored the project concept 68 out of 100 before anything was written, and named what a committee would object to, with all of those fixes going into the submitted version. That is a story about catching problems early, not about producing a weak draft.

Everything in the $299 and $449 tiers already exists as a working example from the DAWRUNA submission, which is why you can trust it generates well and cheaply.

One naming note. Reading the ladder as Draft, Competitive, Full is slightly odd, because "competitive" sounds stronger than "full" in ordinary English. It works if "Full" reads as "the full package", but if a customer ever hesitates at that step, rename the top tier to something unambiguous such as "Everything" and the confusion disappears.

## 4. What the customer does

Paste the grant, upload one to three old proposals if they have them, answer about ten questions, choose their outputs and tier, download a folder. Fifteen minutes of their attention.

The intake decides whether this business works, and it deserves more care than the generation prompt. Generic questions produce generic answers produce identical proposals. The questions must force specifics only that organisation can supply: registration number, exact districts, the two partners by name, the thing they did in 2024 with a number attached, who will run this and what they did before. Every specific you extract is both a quality gain and a distinctiveness gain, because no two organisations answer these the same way. Build it as a short conversation that asks a follow-up when an answer comes back thin, not as a wall of form fields.

## 5. The locks, and why they actually hold

You said there can be no two ways about this. So the design principle is simple and worth stating plainly: **a prompt is a request, a database constraint is a guarantee.** Asking a model to "make it different from the others" is a hope with a good success rate, and a good success rate is not what you promised. Everything below moves the guarantee out of the prompt and into infrastructure that cannot be talked out of it.

**Zero, anchor on the organisation, not the grant.** Two proposals converge when both are generic best practice wrapped around the same guidelines. They diverge on their own when each is built from the applicant's real history, geography, partners and staff. So the generator treats intake facts as the spine and the donor's guidelines as the constraint, never the reverse. Most of your distinctiveness is free if you get this right, and it is also most of your quality.

**One, identify the grant.** Every grant gets a stable identity so you can tell when two customers are competing. Derive it from funder, title and deadline, normalised. Because one customer pastes a link and another pastes text, merge candidates on embedding similarity of the guidelines: above roughly 0.93 cosine with deadlines inside seven days, treat them as the same grant. Everything downstream keys off that grant id.

**Two, claim before writing.** Before a sentence is generated, the project is written down as a structured claim drawn from controlled vocabularies: intervention type, delivery method, primary beneficiary, geography, signature mechanic, structural template, opening device, voice profile. That row is filed against the grant inside a transaction, and it is inserted *before* generation starts, not after. This is what closes the race where two customers generate at the same moment.

**Three, the database refuses repeats.** Put real uniqueness constraints on the claims table.

```sql
UNIQUE (grant_id, intervention_type, delivery_method, beneficiary, geography_bucket)
UNIQUE (grant_id, structural_template_id)
UNIQUE (grant_id, opening_device_id)
UNIQUE (grant_id, voice_profile_id)   -- only for customers with no uploads
```

A second organisation attempting the same combination does not get a nudge, it gets a failed insert, and the planner has to go away and produce a genuinely different claim before it is allowed to write anything. Run the reservation at `SERIALIZABLE` isolation so concurrent generations cannot both win. This is the layer that turns your promise from a strong tendency into a property of the system.

**Four, the finished text is measured and can fail.** After generation, score against every other proposal written for that same grant.

| Check | Method | Cap | If exceeded |
|---|---|---|---|
| Repeated wording | Longest shared word run | 25 words | Rewrite that section |
| Section closeness | Embedding cosine, per section | 0.85 | Rewrite that section |
| Whole document | Embedding cosine, full narrative | 0.80 | Throw away the plan, re-claim, rewrite |
| Concept overlap | Claim fields matching | 4 of 8 | Blocked at claim stage, never reaches here |

Three failed attempts and the package is **not delivered**. It goes to a human queue instead. Never ship a document that failed the gate, because the entire proposition is that you do not.

**Five, no cap on how many customers per grant.** An earlier draft of this blueprint limited each grant to about six customers on the theory that it only supports so many distinct strong approaches. That was wrong, and worth explaining because the correct reasoning is also your best sales argument.

The claim space is far larger than it looks. Intervention type, primary beneficiary, geography and delivery method multiply out to thousands of valid combinations for a typical grant, not six. More importantly, the proposal is built from the applicant's own partners, districts, staff and track record, which means each organisation's strongest approach is usually one no other applicant could credibly claim in the first place. An organisation with ten years in Akkar and a standing relationship with three municipalities has a best angle that is simply unavailable to a Beirut media collective. Collisions are rarer than intuition suggests precisely because the product anchors on the applicant rather than the grant.

So never turn away a paying customer because a grant is busy. Turning people away also fails commercially, since nobody waits for a place to open when a deadline is fixed.

What replaces the cap is ordering, which is honest and creates the same urgency in the right direction. Approaches are reserved first come, first served, and the site says so. That pushes customers to buy earlier rather than pushing them away, which is what you want on a deadline calendar. Show how many organisations are already applying through you as information, never as a limit.

Keep one quiet operational backstop. If the planner genuinely cannot find a distinct and strong claim after several attempts, which should be rare, that job escalates to a human rather than either shipping something borderline or refusing the customer. Handle it as an exception in ops, and never advertise it as a rule.

**Six, hand them the evidence.** Every package ships with the check: a one-page summary showing whether anyone else on that grant shares their approach, structure or opening, and the longest wording in common. Write it in plain answers, not scores. "None" and "6 words, we allow 25" mean something to a customer; 0.29 against a cap of 0.80 does not. Keep the numeric thresholds internal.

**Two limits to publish rather than bury.** You can only speak for proposals written through Ktebli, not for the other eleven applicants who never came near you. And occasionally the obvious approach for a grant will already be reserved, in which case say so before payment and show what is open. Say plainly on the site that you serve organisations competing for the same money and that the locks are exactly why. If a customer works that out for themselves instead of reading it from you, you have lost them.

## 6. The voice engine

Extract a structured profile from the uploaded proposals rather than dumping samples into a prompt and hoping. Capture sentence-length distribution, paragraph length, register, how often they hedge, first person against institutional voice, how they handle evidence, habitual transitions, how they open sections, formatting tics. Store it, let the customer see and edit it, and reuse it next time, which is a real reason to come back.

Then run the humanizer pass on top. A voice profile makes it sound like a person; the humanizer pass stops it sounding like a machine imitating one. You already have that ruleset and you have seen it work.

Customers with nothing to upload get a house voice, assigned uniquely per grant, which turns a gap in the product into another lock.

## 7. Build order

You already have Supabase and Vercel connected, so use them. Next.js on Vercel, Supabase for database, storage and auth, Postgres with pgvector for both the grant matching and the similarity gate, and a job queue because a full package takes longer than a serverless request should. The locks need no infrastructure beyond what you are already paying for.

Ship in this order and resist reordering.

Landing page and waitlist first, posted publicly. Twenty replies teach you more than a month of building. Then intake and payment. Then generation of the narrative only, with the voice engine and the claim step already in place, because retrofitting the locks after you have customers is far worse than building them now. Then the similarity gate. Then budget, logframe and the review, which unlock $299 and $449. Then forms, Arabic output, and the saved voice profile.

Roughly two weeks of real work to something that can take money. Do not let it become two months.

When ktebli.com is registered, add it as a custom domain on the Vercel project and the brand URL goes live with no redeploy.

Tiers without a live payment link yet (Competitive, Full) fall back to a prefilled email plus a promise of the payment link within one working day, so no click dead-ends. Site config lives in two script constants: `PAYLINKS` (per-tier Stripe URLs) and `ENDPOINT` (form-capture webhook, currently empty — until it is set, wizard answers beyond what Stripe's own checkout fields collect are not stored anywhere).

**Flow refinements, same day.** Clicking a specific package now skips the package step and starts the assessment directly, with the chosen package and price shown in the wizard header and the back button reading "Change package" on the first question; only the generic "Start yours" buttons open on package selection. The registration number is optional everywhere — wizard and both live Stripe checkouts — because demanding a number people don't carry in their head kills conversions at the door. It remains the KYC and dedupe anchor in the backend: collect it during the post-payment intake conversation instead, and treat a claim as unconfirmable until it is provided and screened. The uniqueness rule on `organisations.registration_number` stays; the row is simply created at intake time rather than at checkout.

**Backend as built, 19 August 2026.** The database layer above is no longer a plan; it is live on Supabase project `Ktebli` (ref `uocauqflcqefgdixbzpf`, Frankfurt, free tier, created after pausing TY Baby which held the second free slot). Four migrations: `core_schema`, `rls_and_claim_functions`, `seed_pools_and_tests`, `advisor_fixes`.

What exists: fourteen tables including `claims` with all four locks as partial unique indexes over live statuses (`hold`,`confirmed`); `fingerprints` with `organisation_id` nullable and `on delete set null`, so the shape survives account deletion exactly as section 11.4 requires; an append-only `events` audit table whose immutability is enforced by trigger; `escalations` with `due_at` defaults matching the section 11.5 service levels; and `grant_merge_reviews` as the 0.85–0.93 human queue with a four-hour due time. Eight structural templates and eight opening devices are seeded, which means lock three has real inventory: up to 64 structure-and-opening combinations per grant before a human needs to add more.

The claim machinery is three `SECURITY DEFINER` functions with execute revoked from every client role, callable only by the API server's service key: `claim_approach` (self-heals expired holds, refuses flagged or refused organisations before touching the locks, returns exactly which lock blocked a rejected attempt, audits every outcome), `confirm_claim` (hold becomes durable on payment), and `release_claim`. Holds expire after six hours unpaid; expiry is self-healing inside `claim_approach`, so no cron job is load-bearing.

Verified by test, not by reading the code: same concept combination blocked (`concept_combination`), same template blocked (`structural_template`), same opening blocked (`opening_device`), same house voice blocked (`house_voice`), second claim by one organisation blocked (`existing_claim_same_org`), flagged organisation refused (`sanctions_screening`), and a fully distinct claim granted. Every one of those attempts appears in the audit log.

Security posture: RLS on all fourteen tables; clients can read only their own organisation's rows; clients can write nothing except their own organisation record (and cannot change their own `sanctions_status`); grants are visible only to organisations holding a claim on them; `fingerprints`, `events`, `escalations`, `grant_merge_reviews` and the pools are fully invisible to clients; both storage buckets are private, uploads confined to the customer's own folder, deliverables written only by the server. The Supabase security advisor runs clean apart from deliberate deny-all tables and `my_org_id()`, which returns only the caller's own organisation id by construction.

**Processing architecture as built, 20 August 2026.** The generation pipeline now exists end to end, implemented entirely on Supabase (no separate server): five Edge Functions (`analyze-grant`, `save-intake`, `stripe-webhook`, `worker`, `order-status`) plus a Postgres job queue (`orders`, `order_proposals`, `job_stages`, `pre_intakes`).

The flow: the wizard analyses the pasted grant live (`analyze-grant` fetches the page, extracts issuer, title, deadline and funding via the LLM, and autofills the deadline) and persists directions and upload names (`save-intake`) before redirecting to Stripe. A live Stripe webhook (endpoint `we_1U6XWaI29MhWkjQfqIKkICbM`, signature-verified, idempotent) turns payment into an order with a per-tier stage list, attaches the pre-payment intake by email match, sends the tracking email, and kicks the worker immediately. Stripe then redirects the customer to `/orders/session/{CHECKOUT_SESSION_ID}`, which resolves to their permanent tracking page at `/orders/<token>` — an unguessable capability URL that works from any device because all state lives server-side; closing the browser interrupts nothing. The order page polls `order-status` every 3.5 seconds and renders each real stage: done with its duration, running with a spinner, pending greyed out, held with a plain-language note, plus signed download links the moment packaging finishes.

The worker runs on two triggers, pg_cron every minute plus an immediate kick from the webhook, so processing starts within seconds of payment, never "tomorrow". Each invocation loops for up to 100 seconds claiming runnable stages through `claim_next_stage()`, a `FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED` claim that enforces stage order within a proposal, one running stage per proposal, and a global cap of six concurrent stages. Stages heartbeat; `reap_stale_stages()` returns orphans to pending (three attempts, then failed). Stage kinds: analyze, then plan (claims the approach through `claim_approach` with all four locks, retrying free template and opening combinations, confirmed on the spot since payment cleared), then the per-tier document generations, review on Full, check (longest-common-word-run gate against every other proposal on the grant, cap 25 words, breach means held for a human), package (markdown to DOCX, budget JSON to XLSX with live formulas, uploaded to private storage with signed URLs), deliver (completion email once the whole order is finished).

**LLM: OpenRouter, model configurable via Vault** (`openrouter_model`, default `google/gemini-3.7-flash`). Chosen on 20 August 2026 from live OpenRouter data, not memory: in the affordable band (output under $4/M), Gemini 3.7 Flash ranked highest on intelligence (Artificial Analysis index 56), carries a 1M-token context that swallows the longest donor guidelines whole, and Google's models are the strongest of the cheap set on Arabic, which the Full tier needs. At $0.375/M in and $1.875/M out, a complete Full package costs roughly $0.10–0.15 in inference; Draft about $0.04. Against a $299 sale that is a fraction of a tenth of a percent, so quality-per-dollar, not dollars, drove the pick. Runner-up on file: `openai/gpt-5.6-luna` ($0.20/$1.20, intelligence 52.3, proven at ~30B writing-category tokens a week) — one Vault update away if 3.7 Flash ever disappoints; `deepseek/deepseek-v4-flash` ($0.14/$0.28, the volume king of writing workloads) is the floor option and a good candidate for the cheap analyze/extract stages if per-stage model routing is added later. **The key is the one thing only you can supply: run** `select vault.create_secret('sk-or-...', 'openrouter_api_key');` **in the Supabase SQL editor.** Current OpenRouter balance at time of writing: ~$7.9 of $10, roughly 60–150 proposals of runway. Until it is set the worker holds jobs and says so plainly; there is no fake progress anywhere in the system. Emails still need `resend_api_key` (and optionally `email_from`) the same way; without them the tracking page still reaches the customer through the post-checkout redirect and nothing pretends to have emailed.

**Timings** (frontier-model latency, per proposal): analyze 30 to 60 seconds, plan 20 to 40, narrative 90 to 180, each additional document 30 to 90, packaging 10 to 20. Draft lands around 4 to 8 minutes typical, best about 3, slow about 15 with retries. Competitive around 8 to 15 minutes. Full around 15 to 25. The longest poles are narrative drafting and, on Full, the review. Customer-facing copy says "usually within the hour" to leave headroom, and the order page shows real per-stage durations so nobody guesses.

**Concurrency:** proposals process in parallel, stages within one proposal sequentially because each feeds the next. Ten proposals from one customer and ten customers with one each are identical to the queue: rows. At the global cap of six with 4 to 8 LLM-minutes per proposal, ten simultaneous proposals clear in roughly 15 to 30 minutes and fifty in 1.5 to 3 hours; the cap exists to respect Anthropic rate limits on a low usage tier and is one number in one SQL function when the tier grows. Failures retry twice then fail visibly on the tracking page; lock conflicts and similarity breaches go to held, the human queue, and are never silently shipped.

**Second build day, 20 August 2026 — revisions, order numbers, real uploads, and an honest review panel.** After the first successful $1 end-to-end trial, ten improvements went in the same day, all deployed and verified live.

*Revision rounds are now a real product feature, not a promise.* Each completed proposal shows a "Request changes" button on the tracking page (visible while rounds remain: Draft and Trial 1, Competitive 3, Full 10, stored per-proposal as `revisions_used`/`revisions_cap`). The customer picks from plain-language options — tone, conciseness, more detail, positioning, a specific section, organisation facts, formatting, other — and can add free text. `request-revision` validates the capability token against the proposal, records the request, appends `revise → package → deliver` stages after the existing ones, flips the order back to processing, and wakes the worker. The revise stage rewrites the latest delivered narrative against the request; packaging stamps files `V2-`, `V3-`… and the tracking page groups files by version with the newest marked latest. The completion email for each round says "Your revised proposal is ready" and points back at the same tracking link.

*Human-readable order numbers.* Every order now carries `order_no` like KT-10041 (a sequence starting at 10001), shown on the tracking page header and footer, in every email subject and body, with a standing line asking customers to quote it when writing to support. The capability token stays the security boundary; the order number is purely for humans.

*The post-payment moment is no longer a dead end.* The redirect page says the work has started, that a tracking email is on its way, and — if neither the page nor the email materialises — gives the support address to write to. Nobody is left staring at a spinner wondering if their money vanished.

*The grant link is analysed the moment it is pasted.* No analyse button: 900ms after typing stops (or on leaving the field) the wizard calls `analyze-grant`, disables Continue while fetching, and rotates staged messages — opening the grant page, reading requirements, extracting eligibility and submission details. Success renders a preview card (issuer, opportunity, what they're looking for, funding, deadline); failure shows a plain-language error with a retry link and the advice to paste the grant text itself instead. The deadline selector is gone from the happy path: the extracted deadline is used automatically, and a manual date field appears only when none was detected. Pasted text (not a URL) never blocks Continue.

*Old proposals are actually read.* Uploads now travel through the wizard itself (`upload-intake-file`, multipart to private storage): .docx text is extracted by unzipping `word/document.xml`, .txt read directly, PDFs stored and honestly labelled "saved (we will read this format soon)". The wizard confirms "We've analysed N previous proposals and will use them to understand your organisation and writing style." In the pipeline, the voice stage builds a profile from the extracted text — tone, sentence rhythm, vocabulary, structure habits, verifiable organisation facts, and an explicit `do_not_copy` list — and every generation prompt carries the guardrail that nothing may be copied sentence-for-sentence; the profile shapes voice, never content, which keeps it compatible with the distinctiveness locks.

*Checkout asks nothing twice.* Both live Stripe links had their custom fields and phone collection removed (the API accepts `custom_fields: ""` where `[]` silently fails); the wizard's answers persist through `save-intake`, whose row id rides to Stripe as `client_reference_id`, so the webhook attaches intake by id first, email-within-48h second. Checkout is now just email (prefilled) and card.

*What the audit found.* Three capabilities the product implied were not actually implemented. *The Evidence Ledger is the anti-hallucination mechanism.* Every organisational fact carries an id, a source type, a source reference, a date context and a status. Generation prompts get the ledger with one rule: if a fact is not in it, it does not exist for this proposal. A revision set to another customer's text was **held** by the similarity gate rather than delivered.

Cited historical statistics from the ledger were flagged as numeric inconsistencies. ## 10. What will go wrong

The failure that ends the business is two customers submitting lookalike proposals to one grant. Section five exists for this, and it is why the grant record and the claim go in before your tenth customer rather than your hundredth.

The failure that quietly kills growth is output that is fluent, on-voice and mediocre. Reviewers can tell, and a customer who does not win neither returns nor refers. This is why the strategic layer, reading how the donor marks you and running the review, is the real moat. Voice matching is table stakes and someone copies it in a weekend. Knowing what a panel actually scores is much harder to copy.

Watch for customers who want facts invented. A funded proposal built on a fabricated capacity claim is a fraud risk for them and a reputational one for you. The intake should refuse to generate track record, staff or partner claims that were not supplied, and the draft should visibly bracket anything unverified. Make it a stated policy.

Two practical ones. Lebanese associations pay slowly and sometimes in cash, so always take payment before delivery. And donor work is politically sensitive, so offer a real delete control and mean it; the fact that you store only abstract claim data and never anyone's text is a good answer when someone asks.

## 11. Hardening: every way this breaks, and what stands in front of it

Sections 5 and 10 cover the design-level risks. This section is the adversarial pass over the whole machine: assume high demand, assume clever customers, assume bad luck on deadline night, and work out what fails first. One honest framing rule before the list: nothing is bullet-proof, and claiming so is how you get hurt. What you can build is defence in depth, where every failure needs three unlikely things to happen at once, plus a written protocol for the residual case so a bad day is an incident and not a scandal.

### 11.1 Ways the lock itself can fail, beyond the database

**The mis-merge, which is the single most dangerous bug in the business.** The uniqueness constraints only protect proposals filed under the same grant id. If one customer pastes the donor's link and another pastes a badly OCR'd copy of the guidelines, and the matcher fails to recognise them as the same grant, both sail through with no lock at all. Everything else can work perfectly and the company still dies from this one bug. Defences, in order: normalise aggressively before matching (funder name aliases, title stemming, deadline windows); treat the embedding band between roughly 0.85 and 0.93 as "maybe the same grant" and route those to a human decision within hours instead of guessing; and re-run the match for every open grant nightly, because donor pages change and a link that resolved differently yesterday may resolve identically today.

**The retroactive merge protocol.** One day you will discover, after delivering to both customers, that two grant records were actually one grant. Decide now what happens, because deciding during the incident produces panic. The protocol: immediately run the full similarity gate across the merged set; if everything clears, log it and move on; if anything trips, the later-delivered customer gets a free regeneration to a new approach plus a call from you personally, before they submit if the deadline allows. Budget for this as a cost of doing business. It will happen roughly never if the matcher is good, and the protocol existing is what lets you sleep.

**Approach squatting.** A consultant, or a rival, or an over-eager customer creates accounts and reserves the three obvious approaches for a grant with no intention of paying, locking the good angles away from real buyers. Defence: a claim is only durable once payment clears. Before payment it is a soft hold with a short expiry, a few hours at most, after which it evaporates. One organisation, one claim per grant, verified against the Ministry of Interior registration number, which you are already collecting at intake.

**The same organisation arriving twice.** Two staff members from one NGO each start an application for the same grant without telling each other. The lock must not fight them, because they are not competitors. Dedupe on registration number: same org, same grant means resume the existing job, never open a second claim.

**The customer edits the delivered file.** You guarantee the package as delivered. What a customer does in Word afterwards is outside your control, and two customers could in principle edit their way toward each other. Defence is evidentiary, not preventive: hash every delivered document and store the hash with the similarity report. If a dispute ever arises about what you delivered, you can prove it to the byte. State in the terms that the guarantee covers the delivered version.

**Style convergence from the model itself.** A model update, or just heavy volume in one narrow sector, can make everything you produce start to rhyme even when the facts differ, and the similarity gate only compares proposals within one grant. Defence: pin model versions and treat an upgrade like a schema migration, run before-and-after style checks on a fixed test set. Keep a rolling corpus-wide similarity sample across grants, not just within them, and alarm if the average drifts up. The voice engine already pushes against this; the alarm tells you if it stops working.

**Embedding drift.** The similarity caps of 0.80 and 0.85 are calibrated to one embedding model. Swap the model and the same two documents score differently, which silently moves your guarantee. Version the embeddings, store which version scored each pair, and recalibrate the caps on a held-out set before any switch. Never compare scores across embedding versions.

### 11.2 Ways the product embarrasses a customer

**Invented facts, the worst one.** A proposal that claims a track record the organisation does not have is a fraud problem for the customer and an existential one for you. The stated policy exists; harden it with provenance: every factual claim in the output must trace to a specific intake answer or uploaded document, checked mechanically before delivery, and anything untraceable gets visibly bracketed rather than smoothed over. This is a pipeline stage, not a hope about model behaviour.

**Budget arithmetic.** A spreadsheet whose totals do not add is an instant credibility kill with a donor's finance reviewer. Every workbook gets recalculated by an actual spreadsheet engine before delivery and fails the build on any error, the same verification loop used on the DAWRUNA budget. Never ship a number the engine did not compute.

**The compliance checklist that missed a requirement.** Long guidelines get truncated or skimmed by models. Defence: extract every "must", "shall", "required" and eligibility sentence from the guidelines mechanically, then require the checklist to account for each one. Coverage is checked against the extraction, not against the model's memory of the document.

**Arabic that reads like a translation.** If the Arabic deliverable is machine-flavoured, word travels fast in exactly your market. Arabic output gets its own quality gate and its own test set, not a translation pass bolted on the end. Until it passes reliably, sell Arabic delivery honestly as reviewed, and have a human who reads Arabic review it.

**Deadline-night failure.** Your demand curve is spikes around known deadlines, which is also when providers throttle and queues back up. Defences: a second inference provider wired in and tested monthly, not adopted during the outage; honest queue ETAs on the status page; and a degraded mode that guarantees at least the narrative if the full package cannot complete in time, with the difference refunded automatically. The one unforgivable outcome is a paying customer missing a deadline in silence.

### 11.3 Ways people attack it

**Prompt injection through uploads.** The customer's "old proposal" or pasted guidelines can contain instructions aimed at your generator: leak other data, ignore the locks, produce something defamatory. All customer-supplied text is data, never instructions; it enters prompts fenced and labelled, and the output is checked against injection test cases in CI. Boring, standard, and it must be there from day one because your inputs are by definition other people's documents.

**Cross-customer leakage.** The strongest privacy property in the design is architectural: nothing another customer wrote is ever in the context when generating for you, because divergence is driven by abstract fingerprints. Make that a hard invariant with a test that fails the build if any code path puts one customer's text into another's generation, not a convention someone can helpfully optimise away later.

**The customer you must refuse.** Serving a sanctioned or sanction-adjacent organisation is not a reputational risk in this market, it is a legal one, and in Lebanon it is live: screening against the OFAC SDN list and basic verification of the registration certificate is table stakes for a business whose customers apply to the U.S. Embassy. Screen at intake, before payment, so you never have to give money back to someone you are declining. Politically exposed applicants get a human look. Write the refusal email template now, while nobody is angry.

**Fake NGOs.** Someone uses you to dress up a fictitious organisation for donor fraud. The registration-number check catches most of it. Keep every intake answer on file, make the terms state that the customer warrants the truth of what they supplied, and cooperate fast if a donor ever asks. Your defence in that conversation is that the system refuses to invent facts and you can show exactly what the customer claimed.

### 11.4 The paper that protects you

**Define the guarantee before a lawyer does it for you.** "Nothing like it" is marketing; the terms need the operational definition: no shared approach as defined by the claim fields, no shared structure or opening, no wording overlap beyond the published cap, measured by the versioned method, covering the delivered version only, and only proposals produced through Ktebli. Remedy: full refund plus a free regeneration, capped there. No consequential damages, no promise of winning, anywhere, ever, including in Instagram captions written at midnight.

**Ownership and retention in one breath.** The customer owns the deliverables outright. You retain the abstract fingerprint per grant, and that retention must survive account deletion or the lock has a hole in it: deleting your data removes every document and intake answer, while the anonymous shape of the claim persists, tied to nothing but the grant. Say this in the deletion confirmation itself, plainly, because the kind of customer who deletes is the kind who reads.

**The refund table.** Decide now: eligibility check fails, automatic full refund before work starts. Similarity gate cannot clear after human review, full refund plus apology. Customer dislikes the writing, that is what revision rounds are for, no refund for taste. Missed deadline that is your fault, full refund regardless of anything else. Publish it; a published refund policy reads as confidence.

### 11.5 Running it hot

**Instrument the invariants, not just the servers.** Uptime dashboards will not save you; these five alarms will. Claim-insert failure rate per grant, because a spike means either the matcher broke or a grant got crowded. Similarity-gate trip rate, because rising trips mean the planner is degrading before customers see it. The 0.85 to 0.93 match band queue age, because a stale queue is a mis-merge waiting to happen. Human-escalation queue depth against deadline dates. And provenance-check failure rate, because that is the invented-facts alarm.

**The human queue is the real bottleneck.** Every backstop in this document ends with "a human looks at it", and at high demand those humans are the constraint. Define the service level now: escalations resolved within four working hours, and within one hour in the last 72 before a deadline, with named cover. If you cannot staff that during a deadline week, cap intake for that grant honestly rather than silently missing the level. Scarcity of your attention is acceptable; silence is not.

**Rehearse the worst Tuesday.** Twice a year, run the drill on staging: mis-merged grant discovered with two delivered proposals, provider outage at deadline minus 48 hours, sanctioned entity found post-payment. The protocols above only work if they have been executed once when nothing was at stake.

**When something does get through.** A collision, a hallucinated fact, a leak, whatever survives all of this: the playbook is the same. Tell the affected customer before they find out, state what happened in one paragraph without hedging, deliver the remedy from the refund table plus the regeneration, and write the post-mortem into this document as a new defence. In a market the size of Beirut, the story that spreads is never the failure, it is how you behaved in the twelve hours after it.
